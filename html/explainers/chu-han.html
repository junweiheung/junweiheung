Chu and Han

In 210 BC the First Emperor of Qin died on the road, and the empire he had built outlived him by three years. Then came the war between Xiang Yu and Liu Bang, the purge of the men who won it, and fifteen years in which Liu Bang’s widow, Empress Lü, ruled China. This follows the histories, not the operas and novels. The timeline goes year by year from 210 to 180 BC. Its backbone is Sima Qian’s Shiji (Records of the Grand Historian, finished around 90 BC)1, especially the annals of the First Emperor, Xiang Yu, Gaozu (Liu Bang) and Empress Lü; for the years of Emperor Hui and Empress Lü the dates follow Ban Gu’s Book of Han2. Each step quotes the original text and says where it comes from; translations are my own, checked against Burton Watson’s3. Where the two histories disagree, or give no year, the card says so. Years are Western; months are those of the calendar of the time, in which the year began in the tenth month, so “the twelfth month of Han year 5” is early 202 BC4. Borders are simplified to the commanderies and kingdoms of the day.

Key findings

  • Qin broke from the top and the bottom at once. Within a year of the First Emperor’s death came the Shaqiu plot and the Second Emperor’s purge, then a mutiny of 900 conscripts at Dazexiang. From his death to his grandson’s surrender took three years.
  • Xiang Yu won nearly every big battle: he destroyed Qin’s main army at Julu and routed a coalition said to number 560,000 at Pengcheng with 30,000 horsemen. But he split the empire into eighteen kingdoms, and within months they were at war.
  • Liu Bang won with a secure base, good advisers and a great general: Xiao He held Guanzhong, Zhang Liang planned and Han Xin fought. Within seven years of becoming emperor, seven of the eight kings he had created outside his own family were dead, in exile or in revolt.
  • Empress Lü held power for fifteen years. She kept the peace at home and with the Xiongnu, and Sima Qian judged the realm calm under her. Within two months of her death, her entire clan had been killed.

Part 1After the emperor

In 221 BC the King of Qin finished conquering the six other kingdoms of China and named himself the First Emperor. He standardised writing, weights and measures and the width of cart axles, built roads, walls and a vast tomb, and divided the land into commanderies run by officials he appointed. The machine ran on law and on labour: hundreds of thousands of men on the Great Wall, hundreds of thousands more on the southern frontier, and over 700,000 convicts on his tomb and palaces.

It was a machine with one driver. When he died suddenly in 210 BC, the succession was settled inside his travelling carriage by a eunuch. Within a year, conscripts were in revolt and the old royal houses were back. The eight years after that are the Chu–Han Contention between Xiang Yu and Liu Bang5; the fifteen after that are the story of how Liu Bang, and then his widow, made the newly won realm the property of one family.

Part 2The cast

Six people carry the story here. Liu Bang and Xiang Yu fought for the empire. Zhang Liang was Liu Bang’s strategist, Han Xin won half his wars for him, and Fan Zeng was Xiang Yu’s “Second Father”. Lü Zhi was Liu Bang’s wife, who as Empress Dowager ruled for fifteen years after him. Pick anyone on the chart to follow them through the timeline.

Part 4History and legend

Few stretches of Chinese history have given the language more idioms or the stage more plays: sinking the boats, the feast at Hong Gate, Chu songs on every side, the hegemon’s farewell to his concubine, repairing the plank roads while slipping out by Chencang. Some are in the Shiji almost word for word. Others were added much later.

Part 5Rest for the people

The cost of the war is hard to count, but the Shiji leaves clues9. Passing through the county of Quni, Liu Bang asked how many households it had. Over 30,000 under Qin, he was told; so many had died or fled in the wars that there were now 5,000. The Shiji’s treatise on the economy says that in the first years of Han the emperor could not find four horses of the same colour for his carriage, and some generals and ministers rode in ox carts.

So the early Han rulers chose to do less. The land tax was cut to one-fifteenth of the harvest; Cao Shen kept Xiao He’s laws unchanged; Empress Lü abolished the punishment of whole families and the crime of “heterodox speech”; Emperor Hui lifted the ban on books. Sima Qian spares Lü Zhi nothing as a person: the “human swine”, the poisoned and starved princes. But of her rule he wrote something unexpected. The quiet she kept laid the ground for the prosperous reigns of Emperors Wen and Jing.

Emperor Hui sat with folded hands and Empress Lü ruled from the women’s quarters, and the realm was at peace. — Sima Qian
