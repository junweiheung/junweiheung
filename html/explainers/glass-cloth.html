The Cloth Inside the Chip

Under every advanced AI processor sits a sheet of woven glass thinner than paper. One Japanese textile company, founded as a silk spinner in 1918, reportedly makes about 90% of the best grade. Nvidia, Microsoft, Google and Amazon are reportedly queuing for it.

This is about one material, glass cloth, and mostly one company, Nitto Boseki ("Nittobo"). Nittobo's share figures come from industry researchers (chiefly TrendForce) and trade press, not from audited filings, so I say "reportedly" or "an estimate" where that applies. Its expansion plans come from the company's own August 2025 filing. Where sources disagree on dates or prices, I give the range. Yen and US-dollar figures are converted at the mid-November 2026 rates of about ¥159 and S$1.29 per US dollar, and are rounded. This explains an industry; it isn't investment advice.

Key findings

  • Advanced AI chips sit on a package substrate whose core is resin reinforced with woven glass cloth. The grade that keeps big, hot packages flat is called T-glass.
  • TrendForce estimates Nittobo holds about 90% of T-glass and 60–70% of the next grade, NER glass. Trade press calls it the only firm able to make the top grade stably at scale.
  • Nittobo is spending about ¥15 billion (about US$94 million, S$121 million) on a new Fukushima building that could, on its own filing's terms, take output to roughly three times today's level. Relief is not expected before mid-2027, and some forecasters say 2028.
  • Meanwhile, laminate lead times reportedly stretched from 8–10 weeks to over 20, and Nvidia reportedly went to the cloth maker directly. That is unusual this far upstream.

Part 1The cloth nobody sees

Start with why you've never heard of it. Glass cloth is a component of a component of a component. It is woven into the core of a substrate, which holds a chip package, which sits on a board, which goes into a server. Nobody buys it. Nobody puts it on a slide. It doesn't show up in a teardown photo unless you slice the package open.

It is also a small business. BofA analyst Takashi Enomoto estimates Nittobo's whole electronic-materials segment made about ¥40.9 billion (about US$257 million, S$331 million) in 2025, according to Tom's Hardware. Nvidia's fiscal-2026 revenue was US$215.9 billion. Different year-ends, so treat it as rough, but that is about one-thousandth. A very small supplier is holding up a very large industry.

Part 2Inside the package

Here is where the cloth sits. Scroll through the layers of a simplified AI accelerator, from the chip you've heard of down to the layer you haven't.

Layer 1 · The chip
The processor and its stacked high-bandwidth memory. This is the layer with the famous names and the big margins. It cannot work alone.
Layer 2 · The interposer
A slab of silicon that wires the processor to its memory. Nvidia's data show interposers growing from 814 mm² in Hopper to 1,700 mm² in Blackwell, per Tom's Hardware.
814 → 1,700mm²
Layer 3 · The substrate core
Underneath is an organic substrate. Its core is resin reinforced with woven glass cloth. For big, hot AI packages, that cloth is T-glass, with low thermal expansion. This is Nittobo's territory.
~90%T-glass share, est.
Layer 4 · The board
Below that, the circuit board carries signals at high speed. Low-dielectric glass cloth helps there. Nittobo says its version has gone into AI-server switch boards since summer 2023. TrendForce estimates its share of that NER grade at 60–70%.
Why it matters · Heat
Run the chip and everything heats. Silicon barely expands; plain resin expands a lot. Where materials disagree, the package bends. A warped substrate can crack tiny solder joints, so yield falls. T-glass is there to stop the bend.

Part 3Why bigger chips need it

Nobody needed to think about glass cloth when chips were small. Cheap E-glass, the standard grade, is used in lower-end parts, such as microcontrollers and older mobile processors. The trouble is that AI chips keep growing, and a bigger package has more room to warp.

Substrate makers run on thin margins, says Hachemi, so a modest rise in demand can tip them into shortage. Then there is the second effect: newer racks reportedly use more layers and more boards. TrendForce points to Nvidia's Rubin generation, whose substrate has seen "significant increases in both area and layer count", and to cable-free rack designs that need extra midplanes and backplanes.

So each package needs more cloth, and there are more packages. Demand grows on both axes. Supply, as the next part shows, grows on neither.

Part 4One company, one town

Nittobo is a century-old Japanese company. According to a Wikipedia entry and a company-history summary, it was founded in April 1918 as a silk spinner in the city of Fukushima, and by 1938 it had become the first in Japan to industrialise glass fibre. It is now headquartered in Tokyo, but the plant that matters for this story is still in Fukushima.

Why can't others just copy it? According to Tom's Hardware, Nittobo has a proprietary glass composition and a direct-melting production method that rivals have struggled to replicate. Taiwan Glass, Grace Fabric and several Chinese makers are trying, but low yields have so far deterred meaningful entry. A November 2025 TrendForce news item, citing Taiwanese press, calls Nittobo the only company able to stably mass-produce top-grade T-glass.

A recipe anyone can read is not a moat. A recipe that only works at scale in one factory is.

Part 5The queue

When one maker supplies almost everything, demand doesn't compete on price first. It competes on place in line. Nikkei, as relayed by TrendForce, reported that Nvidia, Microsoft, Google and Amazon had booked most of Nittobo's premium cloth capacity for 2026. Yole's Hachemi told Tom's Hardware that Nvidia contacting an upstream supplier like Nittobo directly is unprecedented in this part of the chain.

The squeeze has spread beyond T-glass. MPBxChange's roundup collects supply-chain reports that thin E-glass styles, such as 1080 and 2116, were the tightest by late October, with a survey cited by Commercial Times putting the cumulative rise in style 1080 cloth at about 300% from low to high. Glass cloth is about 19% of laminate cost, per TrendForce, so price rises feed through. The same roundup says weaving looms are a binding constraint: global output of the relevant looms is about 2,400 a year, possibly 3,600 in 2027, according to supply-chain sources. Treat those as reported, unaudited figures.

Part 6The fix, and why it's slow

Nittobo is building. On 29 August 2025 its board approved a new two-storey building at its Fukushima Enterprise Center, with new equipment, for about ¥15 billion (about US$94 million, S$121 million). Japan's trade ministry is expected to chip in up to about ¥2.4 billion (about US$15 million, S$19 million) under a plan to ensure T-glass supply. Construction runs October 2025 to December 2026, with production due to start in the fourth quarter of fiscal 2026, meaning January to March 2027.

It is also adding a second source for the weaving step. Nan Ya Plastics, part of Taiwan's Formosa Plastics Group, said in November 2025 it would weave about 20% of the specialty glass cloth Nittobo supplies by 2027, with Nittobo supplying the yarn. Tom's Hardware adds that Nittobo is doubling raw-yarn capacity at a Taiwan plant. Note what this does and doesn't do: more weavers help, but the key know-how, the glass itself, stays with Nittobo.

The relief date keeps slipping into the future. Yole's Hachemi expects the imbalance to last until the second half of 2027 at the earliest. MPBxChange's roundup cites two European laminate suppliers expecting no significant easing in glass fabric before 2028. Meanwhile demand is moving too: JPMorgan, per Tom's Hardware, reports rising demand for ultra-thin T-glass, a harder product to make.

One more point. Nittobo's own filing notes that it has a medium-term plan of ¥80 billion of capital spending over four years. A company that dominates one grade has every reason to build carefully. Secondary write-ups say it is also scaling back low-margin E-glass to focus on T-glass, which, if true, shifts the tightness onto the cheaper cloth too.

So the bottleneck isn't a chip or a fab. It's a mill, a glass recipe and a yield rate. Next time a shortage hits something that looks simple, ask two things.

QUESTION 1

Who else can make the top grade at a usable yield?

QUESTION 2
