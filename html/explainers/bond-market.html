One Seesaw, Two Fears: How the Fed Uses the Bond Market

The US Federal Reserve aims for inflation of about 2% a year. When it fears a slump, it makes money cheap by pushing bond prices up and yields down. When prices run hot, it does the reverse. Between 2020 and 2023 it did both, in the space of three years. Here's how one seesaw explains it all.

A plain-English model of how the Federal Reserve uses the bond market, built around the 2020–2023 cycle. It simplifies a lot: the Fed sets a short-term interest rate and buys or runs down bonds, while the yields on longer bonds are set by investors, who also react to inflation, growth and government borrowing. Figures come from the Fed, the US Treasury and the Bureau of Labor Statistics (see Sources). Educational only, not a forecast and not investment advice.

The short version

  • A bond's price and its yield move in opposite directions. Push the price up and the yield falls; let the price drop and the yield climbs.
  • The Fed aims for about 2%1 inflation. It never wants prices to fall; it wants them rising slowly and steadily.
  • Afraid of a slump (2020), it cut its rate to near zero and bought so many bonds that its holdings went from about $4.2 trillion4 to nearly $9 trillion. Bond prices rose, yields fell, and borrowing got cheap.
  • Afraid of inflation (2022–23), after prices rose 9.1%6 in a year, it raised its rate to 5.253–5.5% and let its bond holdings shrink. Bond prices fell, yields rose, and borrowing got expensive.

Part 1The price-yield seesaw

Start with the one rule that makes the rest work. A bond pays a fixed stream of cash. If you pay more for that fixed stream, the return you earn on your money, the yield, is lower. If the bond gets cheaper, the same fixed cash is a bigger return, so the yield is higher. Price and yield sit on opposite ends of a seesaw.

So how does the Fed push on the seesaw? It has two tools. The main one is its policy rate, the interest rate banks charge each other overnight. The second is buying bonds, or letting the bonds it owns run off. The rate works on bond prices in a way that surprises people, so it's worth one worked example.

Part 2Afraid of a slump (2020)

In early March 2020, Covid hit. Factories closed, people stayed home, and a deep recession looked certain. The Fed's fear was a slump, and in the worst case prices falling outright, which makes debts harder to repay and makes people delay spending. Its answer was to make money as cheap as it could.

3 and 15 March 2020

The Fed cuts its rate twice in two weeks, from 1.5–1.75% to 0–0.25%2, close to zero.

The dash for cash

Panicked investors sell almost everything, even US government bonds, to raise cash. Bond prices fall: the 10-year yield jumps from 0.54%5 to 1.18% between 9 and 18 March. The market itself is pushing the seesaw the wrong way.

23 March: quantitative easing

The Fed promises to buy bonds in whatever amounts are needed. It pays with newly created money, so its holdings swell from about $4.2 trillion to $7 trillion by June.

The seesaw

Price ↑ · yield ↓ · the 10-year hits a record low of 0.52% on 4 August

With the Fed buying, bond prices rose and yields fell. Cheap money then had two effects worth separating.

Investors cheer

With bonds paying almost nothing, investors look for returns elsewhere. Stocks rise.

The lending catch

Banks are flush with cash to lend, and mortgages get cheap. But nervous households and firms hang back; the bold ones grab cheap loans.

Why "quantitative easing"?When the Fed's rate is already near zero, it can't cut much further. So it adds money another way: it creates new money (reserves held at the Fed) and uses it to buy bonds. That lifts bond prices, holds yields down, and leaves banks with cash to lend. It's "quantitative" because the Fed targets a quantity of purchases rather than a price.

Part 3The turn (2021–22)

Cheap money worked, and then some. Spending came back faster than factories and shipping could. Governments had handed out support, and the war in Ukraine pushed up energy and food. US consumer prices went from rising 0.1% a year in May 2020 to 9.1% in June 2022, the fastest since 1981. Now the Fed's bigger fear flipped.

The Fed stopped buying bonds in March 2022 and started raising its rate the same month. Bond investors didn't wait. Expecting higher rates and worried that inflation would eat into fixed payments, they sold, and bond prices fell before most of the hikes had even happened. Look at the chart: the 10-year yield (gold) starts rising in early 2022, alongside the Fed's rate (black).

Part 4Afraid of inflation (2022–23)

The Fed's goal now was to slow price rises back towards 2%, not to make prices fall. That's sometimes called disinflation. To get there it pushed the seesaw the other way, with both tools.

March 2022 to July 2023

Eleven rate rises take the Fed's rate from near zero to 5.25–5.5%, its fastest increase since the early 1980s.

From June 2022: quantitative tightening

The Fed lets the bonds it owns mature without buying new ones, shrinking its holdings from about $9 trillion. It mostly doesn't sell; it stops replacing.

The seesaw

Price ↓ · yield ↑ · the 10-year peaks at 4.98% on 19 October 2023

People borrow and spend less

Mortgages and company loans get expensive. Spending and hiring slow, and price rises ease: inflation is back to 3.0% by June 2023. That's the point.

From here the story can go two ways, and the same policy looks either like a triumph or a mistake.

Soft landing

Inflation cools without a deep recession or a jump in unemployment. Investors relax, long-term yields drift down, and stocks recover.

Hard landing

Higher rates bite too hard. Firms cut jobs, borrowers default, and stocks fall. In 2022, stocks and bonds fell together.

Part 5Side by side

Strip away the events and the two playbooks are mirror images.

Price up, yield down. Price down, yield up. Everything the Fed does to steer inflation is a choice about which way to tip that one seesaw.

Words you'll see in the headlines
Yield
The yearly return a bond pays, as a percentage of what you paid for it.
Hike / cut
The Fed raising or lowering its policy rate.
Hawkish / dovish
Leaning towards higher rates to fight inflation (hawkish), or towards lower rates to support jobs and growth (dovish).
Liquidity
How easily money moves: how quickly you can turn something into cash, or how easy it is to borrow.
QE / QT
Quantitative easing: the Fed creating money to buy bonds. Quantitative tightening: the Fed letting its bonds mature without replacing them.
Soft landing
Cooling inflation without causing a recession.
