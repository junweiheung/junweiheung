The Gate That Didn't Open

In January 2025, four men stood in the White House and promised US$500 billion of AI data centres. Twenty-two months later, one Texas campus is running, the joint venture behind the name has barely started, and the building happens under other contracts. This is how the biggest number in tech shrank.

"Stargate" means three things: a joint venture (Stargate LLC), a brand OpenAI puts on its data-centre deals, and a political announcement. I count something as Stargate if OpenAI or its partners called it that in public. Much of the 2026 reporting comes from Bloomberg, The Information and the Wall Street Journal; where I've relied on write-ups of their reporting, I say "reportedly". Site status comes from company statements where they exist and independent trackers where they don't. US-dollar figures are converted at about S$1.29 per US dollar (mid-November 2026), rounded. This explains what happened; it isn't investment advice.

Key findings

  • Stargate launched in January 2025 as a US$500 billion (about S$645 billion), four-year pledge from OpenAI, SoftBank, Oracle and MGX, with US$100 billion said to be available immediately.
  • By September 2025 it had grown on paper to nearly 7 GW of planned capacity and over US$400 billion across six US sites, with a 10 GW target.
  • By February 2026 the joint venture itself reportedly had no staff and no data centres under construction. Most real building ran through separate OpenAI–Oracle and OpenAI–SoftBank deals.
  • In spring 2026, an Abilene expansion was dropped, Stargate UK was paused and Stargate Norway went to Microsoft. OpenAI says it has still secured over 8 GW of compute, mostly outside the joint venture.
  • The Abilene flagship was due to be finished by mid-2026. By November 2026, trackers count four to six of its eight buildings live.

Part 1The promise

The day after his inauguration, Donald Trump stood in the Roosevelt Room beside Sam Altman of OpenAI, Masayoshi Son of SoftBank and Larry Ellison of Oracle, and announced the largest AI infrastructure project in history. The name started as an internal code name: Altman has said an early data-centre layout looked like the gate from the Stargate TV series. The number was US$500 billion over four years.

No government money was involved. The cash was meant to come from the companies themselves, with SoftBank in charge of the finances and OpenAI in charge of operations. Abu Dhabi's MGX came in as a fourth backer.

Even the number grew in the room. Son later told Bloomberg that after the election he had promised Trump US$100 billion. Trump told him to go for 200. He came back with 500. Asked how it added up, Altman said it covered the capacity OpenAI expected to need for the next few years, and admitted he wasn't sure even a trillion dollars could be spent profitably that fast.

Within a day, people were asking whether the money existed. Elon Musk posted that the backers "don't actually have the money". Microsoft's Satya Nadella, whose company was missing from the stage, had a shorter answer.

"I'm good for my $80 billion."Satya Nadella, Microsoft, January 2025

Part 2The land grab

Why so big? Because OpenAI kept running out of chips. When ChatGPT's new image generator went viral in March 2025, the Studio Ghibli craze, Altman said the company had to borrow computing power from its own researchers and slow down other features to keep up. His argument for building anyway: if AI cost a tenth as much, people would use it twenty times as much, so OpenAI would still need twice the compute.

For most of 2025, Stargate looked unstoppable. Scroll through the year it grew.

Part 3The power problem

Chips are only half of a data centre. The other half is electricity, and AI needs far more of it than anything that came before. Crusoe's founder, Chase Lochmiller, told Bloomberg that power has become "the major bottleneck".

That's why Stargate went to West Texas. Abilene sits among wind farms that produce more power than the area uses, so a data centre there buys cheap electricity that would otherwise go to waste. Even so, Crusoe is building a gas power plant on site as backup. The cooling system is closed-loop: it is filled with about a million gallons of water once, instead of evaporating that much every day.

Every gigawatt a site like this adds lands on a power grid that is already stretched, and not every country has spare wind. When OpenAI later paused Stargate UK, high energy costs were the first reason it gave.

Part 4Who was paying?

Here's the part the press conference skipped. A data centre this size costs tens of billions of dollars before it earns anything. Somebody has to borrow that money, and lenders want to know who pays it back.

That mismatch is the whole risk in one picture. If OpenAI's revenue grows as fast as it hopes, everyone gets paid. If it doesn't, Oracle is left with buildings full of chips and a lot of debt. Lenders noticed. The price of insuring Oracle's debt against default climbed to a record in July 2026, roughly four times its level a year earlier.

One group gets paid up front, whatever happens next: the chipmakers. Bloomberg's reporter on the story put Abilene's 400,000 chips at a conservative US$15–20 billion, most of it to Nvidia. And the town took a risk too. To win the project, Abilene agreed to waive 85% of the property tax on the data centre. The mayor's view was that 15% of billions is still a lot of new money for roads and police. No one would give him a firm number for how many permanent jobs it would create.

Part 5The stall

A year after the White House event, The Information reported that the Stargate joint venture had hired no one and wasn't developing any data centres itself. The partners had reportedly spent months arguing over who would own and control the sites.

The building that did happen mostly ran around the joint venture: direct contracts between OpenAI and Oracle, and between OpenAI and SoftBank's energy arm. Stargate the brand kept going. Stargate the company barely started. Then, within a few weeks in spring 2026, the retreats began.

The warnings were there in 2025 In January 2025, China's DeepSeek showed a leading AI model could be built with far less computing power, and people started asking whether projects this big were needed at all. Microsoft paused or cancelled some of its own data-centre leases. Tariffs raised the cost of the steel, aluminium and chips a data centre is made of. OpenAI had lost about US$5 billion in 2024. And when Bloomberg asked Altman what could go wrong, he named it himself: that people might stop wanting to pay for AI services.

2026

The money question goes internal

The Wall Street Journal reports that OpenAI missed internal revenue and user targets, and that its finance chief worried about paying for future compute contracts. Altman and the CFO call the report's framing "ridiculous". It is close to the risk Altman named a year earlier.

Disputed

Nobody cancelled Stargate. It got smaller every time someone had to sign a cheque.

Part 6What's left standing

Here's every site that has carried the Stargate name, and where it stands as far as public sources show. Filter by status.

Start with the one site that's real. In 2025, Crusoe said all eight Abilene buildings would be done by mid-2026. That date has passed, and independent trackers count four to six of them live. That's fast by normal standards, and a long way short of ludicrous.

OpenAI's own scorecard looks very different. In April 2026 it said it had secured more than 8 GW towards its 10 GW US goal, with over 3 GW added in 90 days. Both accounts can be true. OpenAI is getting compute, just not mainly through the venture announced at the White House.

So Stargate didn't fall the way companies usually fall. There was no bankruptcy and no single cancellation. What collapsed was the idea that one joint venture, with one headline number, would build America's AI backbone. What replaced it is messier: dozens of separate leases, loans and rental deals, each priced by lenders who care about one thing, whether OpenAI's revenue can cover the bill.

There were two bottlenecks all along: the power to run the chips, and someone willing to carry the debt for them.
