What's a Bond Worth?

A bond is a promise to pay fixed amounts on fixed dates. Working out what that promise is worth today explains almost everything about bonds: why prices fall when rates rise, why a "risk-free" Treasury can lose you money, why a 9% corporate bond may not return 9%, and why some bonds swing far more than others. Eight calculators, one idea at a time.

This explains how bonds are priced, with the formulas written out and a calculator for each. The numbers in the calculators are round and illustrative, not market quotes; real bonds also have fees, taxes, accrued interest and day-count rules that are left out here. For what bonds are and why governments issue them, see the companion pieces on government bonds and the Fed and the bond market. This is educational, not investment advice.

Key findings

  • A bond's price is the present value of the cash it promises: each coupon and the final repayment, discounted at the market's required return, the yield to maturity.
  • If a bond's coupon is above the market yield it sells at a premium, above face value; below, at a discount; equal, at par. As maturity nears, every bond's price is pulled towards par.
  • Holding to maturity locks in the yield you bought at. Selling early exposes you to rate changes: even a Treasury, free of default risk, can lose money if rates rise.
  • A company's promised yield overstates what you should expect to earn, because some borrowers default. The credit spread over Treasuries pays for that risk.
  • Duration measures how much a bond's price moves when rates move: roughly, a duration of 10 means a 1-point rise in rates cuts the price by about 10%. Longer maturities and smaller coupons mean higher duration.

Part 1A bond is a timeline

Strip away the jargon and a bond is a list of payments with dates attached. You pay a price today. The borrower then pays you interest, the coupon, at regular intervals, and on the last date repays the face value, usually quoted as $100 or $1,000. The coupon rate is stated per year, but many bonds pay it in halves: US Treasury notes and bonds pay interest every six months1. So a 4% bond with a face value of $100 pays $2 twice a year.

Coupon per payment = (annual coupon rate ÷ payments per year) × face value. The last payment is that coupon plus the face value.

Part 2Zero-coupon bonds

The simplest bond pays no coupon at all. A zero-coupon bond is sold below its face value and repays the full face value at maturity; the gap is your interest. US Treasury bills work this way. Its price is the face value discounted back over the years to maturity:

Price = Face value ÷ (1 + YTM)n

Turn it around and you can read the yield off the price: YTM = (Face value ÷ Price)1/n − 1. The yield to maturity is the single annual rate that makes the price you pay today grow into what you're promised.

Line up the yields of zero-coupon bonds of different maturities and you get the yield curve. Normally longer maturities pay more, so the curve slopes upward: lenders want extra to tie money up for longer, and an upward slope can also reflect expectations that rates or inflation will rise. The US Treasury publishes the curve every business day2. When short rates are above long ones, the curve is "inverted", which has often come before recessions.

Part 3Pricing a coupon bond

A coupon bond is a bundle of zero-coupon payments: each coupon and the final repayment are discounted separately and added up. Because the coupons are equal, they can be valued together as an annuity:

Price = Coupon × [1 − (1 + y)−n] ÷ y + Face ÷ (1 + y)n

Here y is the yield per period and n the number of periods. The yield to maturity is the rate that makes this equal the bond's market price3. Three cases follow directly. If the coupon rate is above the yield investors demand, they'll pay more than face value for it: a premium. If it's below, the bond sells at a discount. If they're equal, it sells at par4.

Part 4Pull to par

Whatever a bond trades at today, on its last day it's worth exactly its face value, because that's what's about to be paid. So as maturity approaches, a premium bond drifts down towards par and a discount bond drifts up, even if market yields never change. Near the end, the repayment of face value is so close that it dominates the price. This is the pull to par.

Part 5Selling before maturity

Buy a bond and hold it to the end, and, as long as the borrower pays and you can reinvest the coupons at the same rate, you earn the yield to maturity you bought at. Your internal rate of return, the actual annual return on the cash you put in and got out, equals that yield.

Sell before maturity, though, and the price you get depends on yields on the day you sell. If yields are unchanged, your return still equals the original yield. If yields have fallen, the price has risen and you earn more. If yields have risen, the price has fallen and you earn less, and over a short holding period you can lose money outright.

This is why "risk-free" needs care. A US Treasury carries essentially no risk that the government won't pay, but it carries plenty of interest-rate risk if you might need to sell. Silicon Valley Bank learned this in 2023: it held long-term government bonds that lost value as rates rose, and when depositors ran it had to sell them at a loss5. The full story is in the bank-run explainer.

Part 6Short or long?

Suppose you want to lend for two years. You could buy a two-year bond and lock in its yield. Or you could buy a one-year bond, then roll the money into another one-year bond next year, at whatever the rate is then. If investors are willing to hold both, the two plans must be expected to end up in the same place:

(1 + r1) × (1 + f) = (1 + y2)2

Here r1 is today's one-year rate, y2 today's two-year yield, and f the one-year rate a year from now that would make you indifferent: the forward rate. It's the market's break-even guess for next year's rate, before any extra reward investors demand for lending longer. The same logic extends to any number of years: (1 + r1)(1 + r2)…(1 + rn) = (1 + yn)n.

Part 7Credit risk

Companies can fail, so they have to pay more than the government to borrow. The difference between a corporate bond's yield and a Treasury of the same maturity is its credit spread. Rating agencies grade borrowers from AAA, the safest, through AA, A and BBB, the lowest "investment grade", to BB and below, "high yield" or "junk"; the lower the rating, the wider the spread. The saver's ladder tells the story of Hyflux, whose 6% securities showed what that extra yield can be paying for.

The trap is that the yield to maturity is a promised return: what you earn if every payment arrives. To find the return you should expect, allow for the chance of default, and for what you'd recover if it happens:

Expected payout = (1 − PD) × Face value + PD × Recovery

Recoveries vary a lot. Moody's has found that holders of senior unsecured corporate bonds recovered about 49 cents on the dollar on average over 1987–2008, but only 38 cents in the 2009 recession, when defaults peaked6.

Credit spreads are published daily; the St Louis Fed's FRED database tracks them for each rating band7. They widen sharply in crises, when investors fear defaults and want to be paid more to bear them.

Part 8Duration

Some bonds barely move when rates change; others swing wildly. Duration measures that sensitivity. Formally it's the average time until you receive the bond's cash, weighted by the present value of each payment, but its practical use is a rule of thumb8:

% change in price ≈ − Duration × change in yield

A bond with a duration of 10 loses about 10% if yields rise by one percentage point. Two things raise duration. A longer maturity pushes the cash further into the future, where discounting bites hardest. A smaller coupon means less cash arrives early, so more of the value sits in the distant repayment. A long zero-coupon bond has the highest duration of all: equal to its maturity.

That's why banks, pension funds and insurers manage duration closely: they try to match the duration of their bonds to the timing of what they owe, so a change in rates moves both sides together. Shorter-duration bonds are the tool for anyone who might need the money back before maturity.

Part 9When governments print

A government that borrows in its own currency has an option no company has: it can always create the money to pay. So it may never default outright, but it can pay you back in money that buys less, a soft default through inflation. The government-bonds explainer sets out the ways out of a big debt; Greece shows the other side, a country that borrowed in euros and couldn't print them, and so did default.

For a bondholder, what matters is the real return, after inflation. A quick approximation, the Fisher relation:

Real return ≈ Nominal yield − Inflation

A 4% Treasury with 3% inflation earns about 1% in real terms; with 6% inflation, it loses about 2% a year. Inflation-protected bonds, such as US TIPS, raise their principal with inflation so the real return is fixed9. For an ordinary bond, inflation is one more reason yields move, and through duration, one more reason prices do.

Every bond question comes back to one: what is a fixed promise worth, given everything that could happen before it's kept?
