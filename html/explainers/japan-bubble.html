The Biggest Bubble in History

Picture 1989. Japan has rebuilt from the war, grown its economy ninefold in a generation, and given the world Mario. Thirty-two of the fifty largest companies on earth are Japanese, and the land under Tokyo's Imperial Palace is said to be worth more than all of California. Then the bubble bursts. Here is how Japan rose so fast, how a loop between land and shares inflated the biggest asset bubble in history, and why the fall turned into two lost decades.

This is an economic-history explainer, condensed from a longer story. Figures are rounded and the causes are simplified: a bubble this large had many drivers, and economists still debate how much each one mattered and how well the later policy responses worked. Company names (Sony, Toyota and others) appear only as examples of the era, not as the subject. Nothing here is investment advice.

Key findings

  • Post-war Japan grew at roughly 10% a year from 1950 to 1973, rebuilding from near-total destruction into the world's second-largest economy. Human capital, exports and a fixed, cheap yen did much of the work.
  • The 1985 Plaza Accord doubled the price of Japanese exports by strengthening the yen. To avoid a slump, the Bank of Japan cut rates to record lows, and the cheap money flooded into land and shares.
  • A feedback loop inflated the bubble7: rising land lifted share prices, which fattened banks' cross-held shares and capital, which let them lend still more against land and shares. Over half the profits of firms like Toyota and Nissan came from speculation, not their actual business.
  • When the Bank of Japan finally raised rates, the Nikkei fell about 60%9 by 1992 and property followed. Zombie loans kept failing firms alive, a bank crisis hit in 1997, and growth averaged about 0.8%10 a year across the lost decades. Japan's response, including the first quantitative easing, became the playbook the US used in 2008.

Part 1From ashes to miracle

In 1945 Japan surrendered in ruins: about a quarter of its structures destroyed, most of its ships gone, its colonies seized, and the country under an American-led occupation headed by General MacArthur. The occupation rewrote the rules to make Japan peaceful and democratic: full demilitarisation, the emperor reduced to a figurehead, a new constitution renouncing war, and a new government.

It also went after economic power. Believing the big industrial groups, the zaibatsu, had backed the war, the occupation passed an anti-monopoly act and forced their shares to be sold off. Workers won the right to unionise, and union membership jumped from about 3.2%2 in 1945 to 53% in 1948. Under land reform, roughly 38% of farmland was bought by the government and resold cheaply to the people who farmed it. These reforms removed the old non-democratic powers, but they didn't fix runaway inflation and weak output, so food was still short.

The Cold War changed America's aim: make Japan a capitalist success and a bulwark against communism. The 1949 Dodge Plan cut subsidies, balanced the budget and slowed the printing presses to kill inflation, and fixed the exchange rate at 360 yen to the dollar3 to push exports. Then war in Korea turned Japan into a supply base for US and UN forces, and demand for its manufactured goods surged.

With few natural resources, Japan grew by investing, industrialising and exporting. In 1960 Prime Minister Hayato Ikeda set a goal to double national income in ten years, and hit it in seven. From 1950 to 1973 the economy grew around 10% a year, the post-war miracle. Education mattered: by 1960 Japan's secondary-school enrolment already beat the 1965 average of other industrial economies, so workers could move from low- to high-productivity work.

Part 2The oil shock and the Plaza Accord

The miracle ended in 1973. Japan imported 99% of its energy4, so when the oil crisis hit there was panic over blackouts and shortages. Soaring oil prices raised production costs and pushed inflation to 24.9% in 1974. Japan eventually diversified its energy suppliers and built up reserves, but investors had seen its vulnerability and lowered their growth expectations. Having largely caught up to the West on technology, Japan's growth stepped down from about 10% to 5% after 1973. Even so, it was now the world's second-largest economy.

Into the 1980s, Japanese firms, Sony, Honda, Nintendo, Canon and more, became global giants, and a property boom took hold. Land ownership carried deep meaning in a country not long out of feudalism and short on buildable, mountainous terrain. From 1956 to 1986 land prices rose about 5,000%5, falling in only one year. Banks lent freely against land, trusting the myth that it only ever rose.

American anxiety grew as Japanese products spread; in Detroit, autoworkers smashed Japanese cars in protest. In 1985, US Treasury Secretary James Baker gathered the leading economies at New York's Plaza Hotel. They agreed to push the dollar down against other currencies, including the yen.

Why the Plaza Accord matteredA stronger yen made every Japanese export nearly twice as expensive abroad, which threatened a recession at home. To prevent it, in 1986 the Bank of Japan held a low interest-rate policy, cutting the rate it charged commercial banks to a record 2.5% by February 1987. Borrowing was cheap, and the new money didn't go into factories. It went into land and the stock market. Cheap money plus light regulation is how the asset bubble began.

Part 3Cheap money and zaitech

Through the post-war years the Bank of Japan used window guidance: it told banks directly how much to lend, and to which industries. In the mid-1980s it relaxed that guidance and urged banks to lend more. They did, reaching for higher-risk borrowers.

At the same time, companies could pad profits by trading securities and stocks, often without paying capital-gains tax, a practice known as zaitech ("financial engineering"). Brokerages offered tokkin funds with guaranteed returns above interest rates, run through special speculative accounts. In 1985 about ¥9 trillion6 sat in these funds; four years later it was as much as ¥40 trillion (around $300 billion).

The numbers ran away. From 1985 to 1989 real-estate prices roughly quadrupled; from 1985 to 1987 asset prices doubled. As shares climbed, companies' speculative profits soared, and for big names like Toyota and Nissan, over half of profits now came from speculation rather than their actual business. Few noticed that ordinary operating profits had actually been falling.

Part 4The loop that fed itself

The property boom and the stock boom then fused into a single, self-reinforcing machine. Investors began valuing companies on their land holdings, treating owned property as "hidden assets" that made the shares look cheap. The more that happened, the more the whole thing spun.

This is the heart of the story: nothing in the loop needed new real wealth to keep going. Higher land lifted shares, higher shares lifted bank capital, more capital meant more lending, and more lending lifted land. Belief did the rest.

Part 5The peak

Even the global stock crash of October 1987 didn't break the spell; Japan recovered best, convincing many that the government would always hold prices up and shares simply could not fall. In April 1988, the abolition of tax-exempt postal savings8 freed about ¥300 trillion (around $2.2 trillion) for new investment. By that year Japan's economy had grown over nine times its 1955 size, while the US had grown about 2.67 times. The war's humiliation felt answered, and economists worldwide praised Japan's performance.

Yet for young people, land prices had risen so far that an average lifetime's earnings could no longer buy even a small apartment in central Tokyo. Some families took out 100-year, multi-generational mortgages. Japanese companies, flush with a huge trade surplus, spent lavishly in America: in 1989 Sony bought Columbia Pictures and Mitsubishi took a majority stake in the Rockefeller Center, feeding American fears of a takeover.

In December 1989 the Nikkei 225 peaked at 38,915, and Nomura Securities predicted it would reach 80,000 by 1995. But Japanese stocks had already risen three times faster than corporate earnings, even counting unsustainable speculative profits. Things were about to turn.

Part 6The burst

Late in 1989 Yasushi Mieno became governor of the Bank of Japan, set on pricking the bubble. On Christmas Day he ordered a rate rise, and the Nikkei peaked days later. Unlike 1929, Japan's crash didn't happen overnight; the bubble deflated gradually.

The Bank of Japan raised rates five times, to 6% by August 1990. Tight money, fading confidence and existing over-borrowing ended both the stock and land bubbles. By 1992 the Nikkei was down about 60% from its peak, and within a few years Tokyo property had fallen about as far. The bubble had to pop, but people lost fortunes, stopped spending, and tipped Japan into recession.

The government fought back with aggressive stimulus, more public-works spending and lower rates from the central bank, and recovery seemed to come. Then, in 1997, a second shock arrived: a banking crisis.

Part 7Zombie loans and lost decades

After the bubble popped, many companies could no longer repay their bank loans. Rather than force the issue, banks gave big borrowers a break, cutting interest or forgiving part of the debt. These loans that were unlikely ever to be repaid became known as zombie loans, worth hundreds of billions of dollars.

With zombie loans eating into their assets, some banks were in real danger, and in 1997 Hokkaido Takushoku became the first big bank to fail. The government had to step in to rescue others. Clearing all the zombie loans off bank balance sheets took six years to a decade, a painful process, but the crisis finally passed. Across the two lost decades, Japan's economy grew about 0.8% a year in real terms.

Japan also fell behind the US in adopting information technology and investing in the digital industry, going from one of the fastest-growing countries to one of the slowest. Its rigid labour market made it hard to hire or fire; the term madogiwazoku ("window-side tribe") described employees kept on but sidelined, sitting by the window with no real work. With firms offering lifetime employment, young workers found fewer openings and less chance to innovate, creating a "lost generation." Some withdrew entirely, becoming shut-ins who wouldn't leave home or join the workforce. Meanwhile the four Asian tigers, Hong Kong, Singapore, Taiwan and South Korea, had copied Japan's early playbook and were now strong competitors.

Part 8Deflation, QE and the lessons

By 1998 people and firms were spending so little that prices began to fall, and Japan entered a deflationary spiral. The usual cure, print money and cut rates to make cash cheap, had already been running for years; rates were near 0% by 1998, yet spending stayed weak and prices kept dropping. Japan was in a liquidity trap, where normal monetary policy had stopped working.

So the Bank of Japan tried new tools. With forward guidance, uncommon for central banks then, it promised to keep rates at 0% until the economy improved. And Japan became the first country to use quantitative easing.

What quantitative easing doesIn QE, the central bank buys bonds and other assets from banks, paying cash. The banks then have fresh cash to lend out into the economy, which is meant to lift spending and prices. In Japan, QE's effect on deflation and growth was seen as modest, and its usefulness is still debated.

The lessons travelled. When the US recession hit in 2007–08, policymakers studied Japan and moved fast: a $700 billion11 bank bailout, rates cut to zero, and, when that wasn't enough, forward guidance and quantitative easing. Without Japan's painful example, the crisis might have been far worse.

A bubble is hard to spot until it bursts, and Japan's shows how devastating one can be. Today Japan faces a different, supply-side problem: a rapidly ageing and shrinking population that strains its economy, and an open question of whether a country can keep growing as its population falls.

Part 9The yen carry trade

Those decades of near-zero rates left a lasting side effect: the yen became the cheapest money in the world to borrow. That gave rise to the yen carry trade, a bet12 that runs on the gap between Japan's rock-bottom rates and higher rates abroad, and it now links Japan's quiet monetary policy to markets everywhere.

The move is simple. Borrow yen almost for free, convert it to dollars, and buy something that pays more, usually US Treasury bonds or other dollar assets. Pocket the difference in interest, and any gain if the yen falls too. For years it looked like free money.

It's the same lesson in a new form. Cheap money that looks free builds up a hidden pile of leverage, and the reckoning comes fast when the rate that made it cheap finally moves. Japan spent the lost decades at near-zero rates to escape deflation; the carry trade is the bill that arrangement can present, not just to Japanese investors but to markets far beyond.

A bubble needs no new wealth to keep inflating, only belief. The hard part isn't the pop. It's everything that comes after.
