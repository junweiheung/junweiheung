Explainer · Economic history · Germany, 1918–1939

The Dark Wizard

In 1933 Berlin had soup-kitchen queues and six million Germans out of work. By 1939 unemployment had all but vanished and Hitler was parading a new army built almost from nothing, by a country short of gold, oil, rubber and iron ore. Much of the financing was engineered by one central banker, Hjalmar Schacht. Here is how the tricks worked, what they hid, and what they cost.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics

This is economic history: it explains how Nazi Germany financed its rearmament, not a defence of the regime that used it. The rearmament it paid for made possible a war of conquest and the murder of millions. Figures for the 1930s are approximate and some come from a single secondary account, as noted.

Key findings

  • Germany’s recurring weakness between the wars was not a shortage of money but of foreign currency. It needed imported oil, rubber, iron ore and food, and reparations and foreign loans had to be paid in gold or foreign money. Every recovery sucked in imports and drained the central bank’s reserves.
  • Hjalmar Schacht helped end the 1923 hyperinflation, when a dollar cost about 4.2 trillion1 marks, then ran Hitler’s central bank from 1933 and his economics ministry from 1934. To hide rearmament he financed it off the books with Mefo bills, IOUs from a shell company: about 12 billion Reichsmarks of them2 by 1938.
  • Full employment without inflation or a currency crisis was bought with controls: frozen wages, price caps, raw-material quotas, a partial default on foreign debt3, licences for every import, subsidised exports, and confiscatory rules on emigrants that stripped Jewish Germans of most of their wealth.
  • The controls did not remove the costs; they moved them, into shortages, stagnant wages, a housing crisis and persecution. Schacht lost the argument over how far to push rearmament, was dismissed in 1939, was held in concentration camps after 1944, and was acquitted at Nuremberg in 1946.

Part 1Two pictures of Berlin

At the bottom of the Great Depression, in 1932–33, close to six million Germans were registered unemployed, roughly 30%4 of the workforce. Six years later, on Hitler’s 50th birthday in April 1939, Berlin staged a vast military parade, and registered unemployment had fallen below half a million. In between, Germany had built an air force and a tank army almost from scratch, in breach of the Treaty of Versailles, while short of nearly every raw material a modern army needs.

The recovery was not unique: America’s was of a similar size. What made Germany’s unusual was doing it without a currency crisis, without visible inflation and without foreign credit, in a country that had twice in fifteen years been wrecked by exactly those problems. The man most responsible for the financial side was Hjalmar Schacht, a banker who had begun his political life as a liberal democrat and was nicknamed abroad the “wizard” of German finance.

Part 2The constraint: foreign currency

To follow Schacht you need one idea: the balance of payments. A country earns foreign currency when it exports and when foreigners invest in it; it spends foreign currency when it imports, and when it pays interest and principal on foreign debts. If more goes out than comes in, the central bank makes up the gap from its reserves of gold and foreign money. When the reserves run out, factories can no longer buy the imported materials they need, and the economy seizes up.

Germany was unusually exposed. It had to import much of its oil, rubber, iron ore, non-ferrous metals, cotton and wool, and some of its food. After 1919 it also owed reparations, payable in gold or foreign currency, and, from the mid-1920s, interest on large loans from Wall Street, London, Amsterdam and Zurich. Any recovery made things worse: people with jobs buy more imported goods, and an army needs imported steel, rubber and fuel. The figure shows the squeeze Schacht inherited in 1933.

Part 3Weimar: printing, then austerity

The squeeze explains both of Weimar Germany’s economic disasters. After 1919 the new republic faced two huge bills at once. The Treaty of Versailles demanded reparations in foreign currency, while taking away industrial regions and the merchant fleet that earned it; in 1923 French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr, the industrial heartland, and its workers went on strike. And the Kaiser had financed the war largely by borrowing from his own citizens, leaving the republic an enormous domestic war debt.

Raising exports fast was impossible, defaulting with Allied armies on the border looked worse, and squeezing imports would have choked industry. The government turned to the printing press. One reading, made in the video this piece draws on, is that this was less a failure to understand economics than a choice of the least-bad option. Printed marks bought foreign currency for reparations and paid workers in the occupied Ruhr, but every round pushed the mark down and prices up. By November 1923 a dollar cost about 4.2 trillion marks. The war debt was wiped out, and so were the savings and pensions of the middle class. How money creation turns into inflation is the subject of The Lord’s Cut.

Schacht, then a bank director and a founder of the liberal German Democratic Party, was appointed currency commissioner in November 1923 and helped introduce the Rentenmark, worth one trillion old marks. A month later he became president of the central bank, the Reichsbank. The 1924 Dawes Plan, which he helped negotiate, cut reparation payments to a level the economy could bear and opened the door to American loans. Germany borrowed heavily, the economy boomed, and for a few years the foreign-currency books balanced.

The Wall Street crash ended that. American loans stopped, protectionism hit German exports, and the reparations were still due. Schacht resigned from the Reichsbank in March 1930 over the terms of the Young Plan, the reparations settlement he had helped negotiate. Chancellor Heinrich Brüning, with printing ruled out by the memory of 1923, chose deep austerity: cutting spending and wages and raising taxes. A poorer population imports less, which saved foreign currency, at the price of mass unemployment. By 1932 the queues at soup kitchens were everywhere, and voters were deserting the centre for the Communists and the Nazis. Schacht himself moved right: in October 1931 he spoke at the Harzburg rally of nationalist parties alongside Hitler.

Ironically, relief was arriving. At the Lausanne conference in July 1932 reparations were cut by about 90%7 to a final payment that was never made, and the government was preparing job-creation programmes. It came too late. In January 1933 Hitler became chancellor, and in March Schacht was back at the Reichsbank.

Part 4The hidden money

Hitler wanted three things at once: jobs, a huge army and no repeat of 1923. With factories half idle and millions out of work, the first step was not mysterious. Like Roosevelt’s New Deal, the regime borrowed and spent to put idle people and machines back to work; when an economy has that much slack, more spending raises output more than prices. In 1933 much of the money went on civilian public works. From 1934 it went overwhelmingly on weapons.

Rearmament broke the Treaty of Versailles, and the regime feared an Allied reaction if it showed up in the budget. Schacht’s answer was to move it off the books. Four large firms set up a company with almost no capital, the Metallurgische Forschungsgesellschaft (Metallurgical Research Company), or Mefo. Arms makers were paid not in cash but in bills of exchange accepted by Mefo, and the Reichsbank promised to buy those bills whenever their holders wanted cash.

The effect was government borrowing that appeared on no government balance sheet. Because the bills paid interest, many holders kept them instead of cashing them in, so most never came back to the Reichsbank as new money, which helped keep inflation down. Mefo bills paid for roughly half of weapons purchases in 1934–36. When rearmament became public in 1935, the secrecy was no longer needed, and new issues stopped in 1938.

Part 5Eight tools

Spending on that scale soon used up the slack. Once almost everyone had a job, more spending would normally have meant rising wages, rising prices and a flood of imports. The regime suppressed all three with controls, some of them designed by Schacht and his ministry, others added by the Nazi state around him. Tap each tool for what it did and the cost it moved somewhere else.

The cost that fell on Jewish GermansSchacht opposed the street violence against Jews mainly because frightened emigrants converting their wealth into foreign currency would drain the Reichsbank. The regime’s answer was to let them leave but not their money. The Reich Flight Tax, a 25% levy on emigrants’ wealth from 1931, was applied from 1934 to anyone with more than 50,0008 Reichsmarks, down from 200,000, and what remained went into blocked accounts that could be converted only at punishing discounts, which reached over 90% by 1939. The state collected about 941 million Reichsmarks through the flight tax alone. Many who could not afford to leave with nothing stayed, and were later murdered.

Part 6Guns instead of butter

By 1935–36 money was no longer the limit; steel, labour and foreign currency were. Every tonne of imported ore for tanks was a tonne not available for anything else, so the controls were turned against the civilian economy. Imports of cotton, wool and leather were cut to save foreign currency, and with clothing prices capped, the result was shortages: the boom lasted barely a year for the textile industry. Steel quotas, smaller local-government budgets and limits on mortgages squeezed house-building, worsening a housing shortage.

Officially the slogan was “guns and butter”. In practice, Hermann Göring said in 1936 that guns would make Germany powerful while butter only made people fat. Real wages barely grew, working hours lengthened, and the gains of the recovery went into the military, not into consumption.

This was not communism: factories stayed in private hands and business profits soared. But ownership came with orders. Firms had to join state-controlled business associations, and owners who refused paid for it. The aircraft pioneer Hugo Junkers was forced out of his own company in 1933; the steel magnate Fritz Thyssen, an early Nazi backer, broke with the regime and ended up in camps.

Part 7The fall of the wizard

Schacht and Hitler wanted different things. Schacht wanted to restore Germany’s pre-1914 strength and then ease off before the economy overheated. Hitler expected a war of conquest in the east and, eventually, with Britain and America, which between them had far more people, land and raw materials; the logic of racing them pointed to ever more armament, not less. In 1936 Hitler announced a Four-Year Plan to make Germany ready for war and as self-sufficient as possible, so that no blockade could starve it as in 1918, and put Göring above the economics minister.

Schacht warned that more spending would bring inflation. He lost. He gave up the economics ministry in November 19379, though he stayed at the Reichsbank, where he still helped the regime: squeezing better terms from smaller trading partners in south-eastern Europe, and absorbing Austria’s gold and foreign-currency reserves after the 1938 annexation. In January 1939 the Reichsbank’s directors sent Hitler a memorandum demanding a cut in arms spending and a balanced budget. Days later Schacht was dismissed.

He stayed on as a minister without portfolio until 1943. In July 1944, after the failed bomb plot against Hitler, he was arrested and held in concentration camps until the end of the war. At Nuremberg the International Military Tribunal acquitted him in 1946, over Soviet objections; a German denazification court then sentenced him to eight years in a labour camp, a verdict overturned on appeal. He later founded a bank in Düsseldorf, advised governments including Indonesia’s and Egypt’s, wrote The Magic of Money, and died in Munich in 1970, aged 93.

The war economy he left behind was pushed much further by Göring and later Albert Speer, through plunder of occupied countries, forced and slave labour, and terror. It was still outproduced: by Britain in some categories, by the Soviet Union in most, and by the United States in all of them.

Part 8What it teaches

Schacht’s “miracle” is often told as financial magic. It is more useful as a demonstration that the hard limits don’t go away. Germany wanted a fixed currency, its own monetary policy and full employment, which is the impossible trinity in its starkest form: the only way to have all of it was to shut down the free movement of money and goods, exactly what Malaysia did on a small scale in 1998, as the Asian crisis explainer describes. And when the money constraint was lifted, the real constraints, steel, labour and foreign currency, simply took its place.

The controls worked in the narrow sense: no inflation in the statistics, no currency crisis, a giant army. They worked by hiding the costs, in queues instead of prices, in frozen wages, in shortages, and in the property of people the state had decided to persecute. And they put enormous power in the hands of a state that was going to war. As the world again splits into rival blocs, the toolkit of import licences, capital controls and directed credit is being taken off the shelf. Schacht’s career is a reminder of what comes with it, and of what happened to a banker who thought he could serve such a state on his own terms.
