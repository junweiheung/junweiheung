Betting on a Fall

Most investors make money when prices rise. Short sellers borrow shares, sell them, and hope to buy them back cheaper. They're blamed when markets fall and, now and then, they're the first to spot a fraud. Here is how shorting works, who lends the shares, how to read the numbers, and why a crowded short can blow up.

Key findings

  • A short seller borrows shares, sells them, and later buys them back to return. The profit is the fall in price, less a borrowing fee. The debt is counted in shares, not dollars.
  • That makes the risk lopsided: the most a short can make is 100%, if the company goes to zero, but the loss has no ceiling.
  • The shares mostly come from pension funds, insurers and index funds, which lend them out for a fee and can ask for them back.
  • Every short must one day be bought back. When too many crowd in, a rising price can force them all to buy at once: the squeeze that hit Volkswagen shorts in 2008 and GameStop shorts in 2021.
  • Short sellers profit from bad news, so they hunt for it. Some of the biggest frauds of the past fifteen years, including Sino-Forest and Wirecard, were called out by shorts before regulators acted. Regulators still ban shorting in a panic, as the US did in 2008, six European countries in 2020 and South Korea from 2023 to 2025.

Part 1How a short sale works

Most investors make money one way: buy, wait for the price to rise, sell. A short seller runs the trade backwards. They borrow shares from someone who owns them, usually arranged by a broker, sell them straight away, and hope to buy them back later for less and hand them back to the lender. The gap between the two prices is the profit. While the loan is open the short seller pays a borrowing fee and owes the lender any dividends the shares pay.

US rules require the broker to have found shares to borrow, or have reasonable grounds to believe it can, before it accepts a short sale; selling without lining them up first is called naked short selling1. The animation below walks through a single share.

Part 2Lopsided risk

That lopsidedness is the first thing to understand about shorting. Buy a share at $100 and the worst case is losing $100. Short it at $100 and the best case is making $100, if the company goes to zero, while the loss has no cap. In practice a short seller rarely rides a loss to the end: the broker holds the sale proceeds plus extra collateral, called margin, and as the price rises it asks for more. If the short seller can't pay, the broker buys the shares back for them, at a loss.

There are other ways to bet on a fall, with different risks, and most of them are derivatives. Buying a put option gives the right to sell at a set price, so the most you can lose is what you paid for it. Selling a futures contract profits if the price drops, with no shares to borrow at all, though the loss is as open-ended as a short sale's. The chart compares a short sale with a put.

Part 3Who lends the shares

Every short sale needs a lender, and the biggest lenders are the most patient investors in the market: pension funds, insurers, sovereign funds, endowments and mutual and index funds. They plan to hold their shares for years anyway, so lending them out for a fee earns a little extra. The borrower posts collateral, usually cash or bonds worth a bit more than the shares, and the lender can recall the shares when it wants them back2.

The money is small but steady. Morningstar estimates that index funds earn about 0.1% a year on average from lending, enough to offset part of their fees. For most large companies, plenty of shares are on offer and borrowing is cheap. For a stock that many people want to short and few are lending, the fee can climb steeply: such stocks are called hard to borrow. Recall is a risk for the short seller too: if the lender wants its shares back and no one else will lend, the short seller has to buy in the market, whatever the price.

Part 4Reading the numbers

Because shorts must one day be bought back, how much of a company is sold short is worth watching. In the US, brokers report their customers' short positions in every stock twice a month to FINRA, the industry regulator, which publishes the totals about a week later3. In May 2026 FINRA proposed collecting them more often. Two numbers come out of it.

Short interest is the number of shares sold short and not yet bought back, often shown as a percentage of the shares available to trade. Days to cover divides that by the average number of shares traded each day: it says how many days of normal trading it would take for every short seller to buy back. The higher either number, the more crowded the exit if the price starts to rise.

Singapore takes a different approach. Since October 2018 anyone whose short position in an SGX-listed stock reaches the lower of 0.2% of its shares or S$2 million must report it to the Monetary Authority of Singapore each week, and brokers must mark every short sell order sent to the exchange4.

Part 5The squeeze

Shorting has a weak spot that buying doesn't. Every short seller must one day buy the shares back, so a crowded short is a queue of guaranteed future buyers. If the price starts to rise, losses and margin calls force some of them to buy, which pushes the price up further and forces more of them to buy. That spiral is a short squeeze, and when there are few shares available it can be violent.

October 2008

Volkswagen

On Sunday 26 October, Porsche revealed that it controlled about 74%5 of VW's ordinary shares: some held outright, the rest through cash-settled options that hadn't shown up in its earlier disclosures. With the state of Lower Saxony holding about a fifth, very few shares were left for the hedge funds that had shorted the stock to buy back.

What happened: the price went from €211 on the Friday to more than €1,000 on the Tuesday, and for a day Volkswagen was the most valuable company in the world.
January 2021

GameStop

Short interest in the struggling video-game retailer had reached more than the total number of its shares available to trade, possible because the same share can be lent, sold, and lent again. A wave of small investors, many organised on Reddit, bought the stock and its call options.

What happened: the price went from under $19 at the end of 2020 to a closing high of $347.516 on 27 January (before a later share split). Melvin Capital, a hedge fund with a large short, lost 53% that month and took $2.75 billion of new money from Citadel and Point72.

The two squeezes had different causes. Volkswagen's came from a buyer quietly cornering the shares through derivatives; GameStop's from crowds of buyers acting in the open. In both, the short sellers' own need to buy was the fuel.

Part 6Short sellers as detectives

Short sellers are often unpopular: they profit when companies fail, and they're easy to blame when prices fall. But they're also among the few investors paid to look for bad news, and some go further. Activist short sellers research a company, take a short position, and then publish their findings, betting that the market will agree once it reads them. That conflict is real, since the authors profit from the fall they help cause, and companies often say so. Sometimes the research is right.

2011 · Muddy Waters

Sino-Forest

A report by Carson Block's firm said the Canadian-listed Chinese timber company had greatly overstated its forests. Sino-Forest denied it and accused the firm of profiting from the fall7.

What happened: the shares fell 64% in two days. Ontario's regulator halted trading that August, the company sought bankruptcy protection in 2012, and the regulator later ruled that its former chief executive and four other executives had broken Ontario securities law.
2019 · Financial Times

Wirecard

After FT reporting questioned the German payments company's accounts, Germany's regulator, BaFin, banned new short positions in it for two months8, the first such ban on a single stock.

What happened: in June 2020 Wirecard admitted that €1.9 billion of cash it had reported probably didn't exist, and filed for insolvency. BaFin was later sharply criticised, including by a parliamentary inquiry, for going after the company's critics.
2023 · Hindenburg

Adani

The day before Adani Enterprises' $2.5 billion share sale was due to open, Hindenburg Research accused the Indian group of stock manipulation and accounting fraud. Adani called it a "calculated attack on India" and denied it9.

What happened: the group's companies lost more than $100 billion of market value within days and the share sale was cancelled. Hindenburg's founder closed the firm in January 202510.

A short seller is paid to doubt a company's story. That makes them biased, and sometimes the only ones checking.

Part 7Rules and bans

The main US rules sit in Regulation SHO. Besides the locate rule, short sales must be marked as such, and if a seller fails to deliver the shares when the trade settles, the broker must buy shares to close the gap, generally by the start of trading on the next settlement day11. Stocks with large, persistent failures go on a public "threshold" list with tighter rules. Naked shorting worries regulators because it can sell shares that don't exist, piling extra selling onto a stock.

In a panic, regulators sometimes go further and ban shorting outright, on the theory that short sellers deepen the fall. The record is mixed. A study of the 2008 US ban found that trading in the banned shares became more expensive, with wider gaps between buying and selling prices, while bank shares kept falling through the weeks it was in force.

For a reader, the useful habit is the reverse of the short seller's: when a company's shares are heavily shorted, ask what the shorts think they know. Sometimes they're wrong and get squeezed. Sometimes, as with Wirecard, they were the only ones reading the accounts.
