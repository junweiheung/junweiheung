The Safety Lab That Became a Juggernaut

In late 2020 seven researchers walked out of OpenAI, worried about how fast and how carelessly powerful AI was being built. Six years later the company they founded is worth about $1.5 trillion, sells the coding tool much of Silicon Valley now works with, and has fought the Pentagon in court. Here is how Anthropic got there, where the accounts of its rise disagree, and the strongest case its critics make.

This draws on four video accounts of Anthropic’s rise, one sympathetic business profile, one explainer, one short Chinese-language video and one fiercely critical Chinese-language video, checked against filings, court records and news reporting. Where they disagree, both versions are shown. A disclosure: this page was drafted with help from Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, the company it describes. Every factual claim is sourced, the critics get a full section, and readers should weigh the conflict of interest. Nothing here is investment advice.

Key findings

  • Anthropic was founded in 2021 by seven former OpenAI staff, led by siblings Dario and Daniela Amodei, as a public benefit corporation. Its early money included about $500 million led by Sam Bankman-Fried, months before FTX collapsed.
  • It chose businesses over consumers, and coding made it. Claude Code, previewed in February 2025, passed $2.5 billion of annualised revenue within a year, and first-half 2026 revenue reached about $16.3 billion, up from $1.2 billion a year earlier.
  • In February 2026 it refused to let the Pentagon use Claude for mass domestic surveillance or fully autonomous weapons, was labelled a “supply chain risk”, and briefly became the No. 1 app on Apple’s US App Store. Courts have since split on the designation.
  • Critics see a gap between its safety language and its actions: it dropped its pledge to pause dangerous development in February 2026, paid $1.5 billion to settle claims over pirated books, and its model has been reported in US military operations. Its defenders say a cautious lab that stops racing simply hands the lead to less careful ones.

Part 1The divorce

Caltech to Google Brain · The scaling hunch

Dario Amodei was trained as a physicist, at Caltech, Stanford and then Princeton. His father’s death from an illness that became curable only a few years later pushed him towards biology, then towards the idea that faster science could save lives, and eventually towards AI. At Baidu’s Silicon Valley lab in 2014–15, working on speech recognition, he noticed what would define his career: feed a model more data and computing power, and it improves in a surprisingly predictable way. At Google Brain he co-wrote Concrete Problems in AI Safety (2016), one of the first papers to treat accidents in AI systems as an engineering problem. His sister Daniela took a different road, through politics, nonprofits and an early job at the payments company Stripe.

Scalingmore data and compute, better models
The other view

A race-maker warning about races

The scaling hunch is the engine of the AI race. Critics say a company founded on making models ever bigger is building the very danger it warns about.

The replyAnthropic’s founding argument is that safety research is only useful on frontier models, so a careful lab has to build them too.

2020 · The divorce

Both ended up at OpenAI. Dario ran safety work and co-led the research behind GPT-2 and GPT-3; in January 2020 he co-authored the paper on scaling laws that turned his Baidu hunch into equations. Daniela became vice-president of safety and policy. By late 2020, a group of senior researchers had fallen out with OpenAI’s leadership. In December they left together, a split insiders called “the divorce”. Exactly why is still argued over. Scroll on to see how far the people who walked out would go.

7co-founders walk out of OpenAI
The other view

Safety, or a falling-out?

The flattering version is a principled exodus over safety. Dario Amodei himself has framed it more as a loss of trust in OpenAI’s leadership, citing how deals and access decisions were handled.

The replySupporters say the two can’t be separated, and only the people in the room know the weighting.

Part 2A lab, FTX, and a constitution

2021 · A public benefit corporation

The seven founders incorporated Anthropic in early 2021 as a public benefit corporation, a company whose charter lets it weigh a public mission alongside profit. Its first $124 million came in a Series A that year, mostly from technology founders rather than venture funds.1

$124MSeries A
The other view

A charter, not a constraint

A public benefit corporation may weigh its mission; it is not forced to. Critics add that the seven founders keep 50.1% of the votes through a single share class.

The replyGovernance experts have praised the structure, with its Long-Term Benefit Trust, as a guard against mission drift.

Theme: a closed, mission-driven culture · raised in 2 of 4 videos

April–November 2022 · The FTX money

Safety research at the frontier needs frontier-scale computing, and in April 2022 Anthropic found the money to buy it: a $580 million Series B led by Sam Bankman-Fried, then the celebrated chief executive of the crypto exchange FTX, whose firms put in roughly $500 million for non-voting shares. It paid for the servers. Seven months later, in November, FTX collapsed in one of the largest frauds in American history. The stake passed to FTX’s bankruptcy estate, which later sold it to repay creditors, and the scandal tarnished effective altruism, the philanthropic movement Bankman-Fried had championed and several of Anthropic’s founders and backers were close to.

$580MSeries B, about $500M of it from FTX
The other view

Funded by a fraud

For critics the episode shows a gap between Anthropic’s caution about AI and its appetite for money: a lab built on spotting catastrophic risk took its largest early cheque from a crypto empire without seeing the risk inside it.

The replyAlmost nobody outside FTX saw the fraud coming, and because the shares carried no votes, Bankman-Fried never had a say in how the lab was run.

From the video accounts

November–December 2022 · Two shocks

The same month brought a second shock. By the videos’ accounts, Anthropic had a capable chatbot by mid-2022 and held it back, wary of starting a race. On 30 November OpenAI launched ChatGPT and started the race anyway. Anthropic’s answer, weeks later, was research rather than a product: Constitutional AI. Instead of relying only on human raters, give a model a written list of principles, drawn partly from documents such as the UN’s Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and train it to critique and revise its own answers against them.2

The other view

Principle, or a lost race?

Holding back a chatbot sounds principled. Industry insiders argue ChatGPT simply won consumers’ attention so completely that Anthropic had to turn to businesses.

The replyAnthropic does run consumer apps, and in 2026 one briefly topped Apple’s App Store. Businesses were a choice, it says, not a consolation prize.

Part 3The enterprise bet

March–September 2023 · Business first

Claude launched in March 2023, first to business partners. Claude 2 that July could read 100,000 tokens at once, roughly a 75,000-word book, which suited companies feeding it contracts and codebases. In September Anthropic published its Responsible Scaling Policy, which graded models by “AI Safety Levels” and committed the company to pause development or deployment if its safeguards fell behind a model’s dangerous capabilities.3 Hold on to that promise; it comes back in 2026.

The other view

A promise it could rewrite

The pause pledge was voluntary, written by the company about itself. Critics note that the company could rewrite it, and in February 2026 it did.

The replyAnthropic argues that a lab pausing alone simply hands the frontier to less careful rivals.

Autumn 2023 · The cloud giants

The same autumn the cloud giants arrived. Amazon agreed to invest up to $4 billion (later doubled to $8 billion), and Google committed up to $2 billion on top of an earlier stake, making Claude a fixture of both companies’ cloud platforms.4 Those two ended up as Anthropic’s largest shareholders, as Who Owns Anthropic? explains.

$4BAmazon’s initial commitment
The other view

Independent, on whose servers?

Much of Anthropic’s revenue flows through Amazon’s and Google’s cloud marketplaces, and both companies build rival models. Critics ask how independent a lab can be when its competitors carry its sales.

The replyBoth deals gave Anthropic the computing power it needed, and Claude is sold on several clouds, not just one.

2024 · The coding model

In March 2024 the Claude 3 family (Haiku, Sonnet and Opus) put Anthropic level with OpenAI on many benchmarks, and in June Claude 3.5 Sonnet became the model programmers preferred, powering coding tools such as Cursor. That October Dario Amodei published Machines of Loving Grace, a long essay making the case for what AI could do for medicine and science: a “country of geniuses in a datacenter”.5 The same autumn Anthropic released “computer use”, letting Claude operate a screen, and the open Model Context Protocol for connecting AI to other software.

The other view

Utopia as a sales pitch

Critics read Machines of Loving Grace and the later warnings as two halves of one pitch: AI is so powerful it must be built, and built by us.

The replyAmodei rejects “doom marketing”, saying his essays propose concrete policy and are often quoted out of context.

Part 4Claude Code

February–May 2025 · Claude Code

The product that turned Anthropic from a respected lab into a juggernaut started as an internal experiment. An engineer, Boris Cherny, built a tool that let Claude work directly in a programmer’s terminal: reading the codebase, running commands, editing files. Anthropic previewed Claude Code in February 2025 and released it fully in May. Programmers stopped asking an AI for snippets and started handing it whole tasks.

The other view

A few customers, a lot of revenue

Critics point out that a handful of customers make up a large share of Anthropic’s revenue, so losing one big coding customer would hurt.

The replyAnthropic says customers who adopt Claude Code tend to spend much more over time.

September 2025–January 2026 · From developers to the office

Developers became Anthropic’s base, and investors followed them. In September 2025 a $13 billion round valued the company at $183 billion, triple its price six months earlier.6 Then Anthropic pointed the same idea at office work. In January 2026 new plug-ins for Claude Cowork, an agent that handles everyday business software tasks, set off a sell-off in software stocks that a Jefferies strategist named the “SaaSpocalypse”: if an agent can do the work, why pay for every seat of the software?7

$183Bvaluation, September 2025
The other view

Warning about jobs, selling the replacement

The company whose chief executive warns about AI wiping out jobs sold the agent that sent software stocks tumbling on fears it would replace paid seats.

The replyAmodei says his essays separate tasks from jobs and propose policy fixes for the transition.

February 2026 · The engine

By February Claude Code alone was running at more than $2.5 billion a year and the company at about $14 billion, and Anthropic raised $30 billion at a $380 billion valuation.8 In under a year a terminal tool had become the engine of one of the most valuable companies in the world.

$380Bvaluation, February 2026
The other view

Whose revenue?

Anthropic counts the full amount customers spend through cloud marketplaces as revenue. OpenAI and other critics say the partner’s cut, roughly 16–20%, should come off first.

The replyAccounting rules allow either, depending on who controls the sale; the dispute is about comparing companies, not a hidden number.

Part 5Power, red lines and the IPO

2025–January 2026 · Washington, and the bill for books

Success put Anthropic in rooms it had once warned about. In January 2026 Dario Amodei published The Adolescence of Technology, a 20,000-word survey of the dangers of powerful AI, from bioterrorism to job losses, the dark twin of Machines of Loving Grace.9 By then his company was already deep inside government. In 2025 it had launched Claude Gov for national-security agencies, won a Pentagon prototype agreement worth up to $200 million alongside Google, OpenAI and xAI, and offered Claude to all three branches of the US government for $1 per agency.10 It had also settled the biggest copyright case in American history, paying authors $1.5 billion after a judge ruled that training on books was fair use but building a library from pirated copies was not.11

The other view

Careful AI, pirated books

Critics see a pattern: a lab that asks the world to trust its judgment took the fast route on training data, then paid its way out.

The replySupporters note that the court backed the method itself: training on books Anthropic had bought and scanned was fair use, a ruling the whole industry now leans on.

Theme: safety talk, racing walk · raised in 3 of 4 videos

February–September 2026 · The red lines

In late February the contradictions met. The Pentagon wanted to use Claude for “any lawful use”; Anthropic would not drop two restrictions, on mass surveillance of Americans and on fully autonomous weapons. The Defense Department designated Anthropic a supply chain risk, a label normally reserved for suspect foreign suppliers, which bars contractors from using its products on defence work. Consumers rallied: Claude overtook ChatGPT as the most downloaded app on Apple’s US App Store.12 Anthropic sued in March. In August a federal judge in California found the designation was unconstitutional retaliation; in September an appeals court in Washington upheld it under a different rule, leaving the company in legal limbo.13

No. 1on Apple’s US App Store
The other view

Principle, or performance?

Pentagon officials and critics say Anthropic had pushed hard to get onto classified networks, and that the guardrails it fought over were already required by US law and military policy.

The replyAnthropic objected to only two uses, and the Pentagon’s own spokesman said it had no interest in either. If the limits cost nothing, critics have to explain why the Pentagon fought so hard over them.

Where the accounts disagree · Part 6

24 February 2026 · The same week: the pause that wasn’t

On 24 February 2026, as the Pentagon pressed, Anthropic published version 3 of its Responsible Scaling Policy. It dropped the 2023 commitment to pause development if safety measures lagged, replacing it with public, non-binding goals. Co-founder Jared Kaplan said unilateral commitments no longer made sense given how fast the field was moving.14

The other view

Brave in one fight, retreating in another

Critics note the timing: the commitment Anthropic had held up as proof it was different went in the same week it was praised for defying the Pentagon.

The replyAnthropic says it lowered no existing safeguards, and that publishing its goals lets outsiders hold it to them.

January–February 2026 · Claude at war

Red lines on paper did not keep Claude out of war. The Wall Street Journal reported that Claude, reached through the data company Palantir, was used in the January operation to capture Venezuela’s Nicolás Maduro; Anthropic said it could not comment on specific operations.15 On 28 February a US missile struck a girls’ school in Minab, Iran, killing well over 100 people, mostly children; the targeting reportedly drew on a Palantir system that includes Claude. Asked months later, Amodei said he did not know what role Claude had played, called the strike “a really terrible thing”, and said the use did not breach Anthropic’s policies because “a human made that final call”.16

The other view

“For all of humanity”?

A company that says it serves all of humanity sought classified military work early and lobbies for tough chip export controls on China. “A human made that final call” is cold comfort, critics say, when the model feeds the targeting.

The replyAmodei argues democracies must keep a lead over autocracies, and that keeping a human in charge of lethal decisions is exactly the line Anthropic fought the Pentagon over.

Theme: military and geopolitical hawkishness · raised in 3 of 4 videos

February–September 2026 · Distillation and Mythos

Anthropic also went on the attack. In February it accused DeepSeek, Moonshot and MiniMax of distilling Claude, training their own models on its answers through some 24,000 fraudulent accounts and 16 million exchanges; a September report named five labs and nearly 200 million exchanges.17 In April it unveiled Claude Mythos, a model so capable at finding software vulnerabilities that it was released only to vetted partners through Project Glasswing, to patch critical systems before attackers could use similar tools.18

The other view

Scarcity as marketing

Withholding Mythos from the public looked to critics like scarcity marketing: a model too dangerous to release makes a powerful advertisement.

The replyGlasswing partners did use Mythos to find and patch real vulnerabilities.

May–November 2026 · The IPO

None of it slowed the money. A private round in May valued Anthropic at $965 billion. Its IPO filing showed about $16.3 billion of revenue in the first half of 2026, a first operating profit of about $974 million in the second quarter, and compute commitments reported at around $590 billion.19 On 18 November it priced its shares at $83, raising $81.3 billion in the largest IPO ever, at about $1.5 trillion. Who owns that company, and why its founders still control it, is the subject of Who Owns Anthropic?.

$1.5Tfully diluted value at $83
The other view

Built on commitments

One video reports that growth slowed sharply in the third quarter. Critics add that $590 billion of compute commitments dwarfs a single profitable quarter.

The replySupporters say the commitments are spread over years, against revenue that grew more than tenfold in a year, with a high gross margin.

    Part 6Where the accounts disagree

    Four narrators have told this story: a business profile, an explainer, a short-form clip and a polemic. They agree on every date above. They split on almost every motive. Was the walk-out about safety or about trust? Was the Pentagon fight a principle or a performance? Pick a question and set the versions against the paper trail.

    Part 7The case against

    By November 2026 Anthropic had the revenue, the valuation and the reputation of the industry’s conscience. That last asset is the one its critics attack, from opposite directions: some say it is not cautious enough, some that its caution is a sales pitch, and governments that it is too political. Here is the prosecution’s case, theme by theme, with the defence and what the record can actually show.

    One video goes much further than the rest. The Chinese-language Claude背后的Anthropic：掌握核心科技的邪教 (“The Anthropic behind Claude: a cult that controls the core technology”) calls the company a cult and compares its “mission, enemy, crisis” framing to the rise of totalitarian movements. That is an argument, not a finding, and some of the video’s specific claims could not be verified, so they are left out here. Even that video concedes that the technology is real and that Claude is, in its view, the strongest model in the world.20

    Part 8Open questions

    Can the structure hold under public markets? Anthropic’s public-benefit charter, its Long-Term Benefit Trust and the founders’ 50.1% voting control were designed to protect the mission from shareholders. Quarterly earnings will test that every three months. As one of the accounts puts it, a principle is not a principle until it costs you money.21

    How does the Pentagon case end? With two courts split, the supply-chain designation may reach the Supreme Court, and with it the question of whether an AI company can set limits on how a government uses what it buys.

    Can revenue keep up with compute? Hundreds of billions of dollars of computing commitments assume demand keeps compounding. Growth slowed sharply between the second and third quarters of 2026, according to one of the video accounts, and much of the revenue flows through Amazon’s and Google’s cloud marketplaces, which count it differently from rivals.22

    Is Anthropic’s founding bet right? Its whole strategy rests on the idea that the safest way through powerful AI is for careful people to be at the frontier, setting norms by example. Its supporters see that bet paying off; its critics see a company that warns about the race while winning it.23 Both can point to 2026 as evidence.
