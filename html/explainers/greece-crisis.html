The Debt That Nearly Broke the Euro

In 2001 Greece squeezed into the euro. Eight years later a new government admitted the budget figures had been wrong, and the country fell into one of the deepest slumps any developed economy has suffered in peacetime. How Greece got in, what it hid, why three bailouts and years of austerity left its debt higher than before, and what the crisis says about sharing a currency.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics
November 28, 2026 · 17 min read

This summarises Greece's debt crisis from its entry into the euro to the end of its last bailout in 2018. Figures for debt, deficits, growth and unemployment are Eurostat's latest revised series, which differ in places from the numbers reported at the time. Why the crisis was so deep is still argued over, especially the role of austerity; where economists disagree, I set out both sides. The structure of the story follows a video explainer1, with every figure checked against official data. This is educational, not investment advice.

Key findings

  • Greece joined the euro in 2001, two years after the first eleven countries. A 2004 audit found its deficits had been under-reported, and that by the revised figures it had never actually met the 3% limit.
  • Inside the euro, Greece could borrow almost as cheaply as Germany. Its debt stayed above 100% of GDP through the boom years, and its deficit never fell below 4%.
  • In October 2009 a new government said the year's deficit would be about 12.5% of GDP, not 3.7%. Eurostat now puts it at 15.4%. Lenders stopped lending, and Greece needed rescues worth €110 billion (2010), €130 billion (2012) and up to €86 billion (2015), plus the biggest write-off of private debt ever made on a government's bonds.
  • The rescues came with deep spending cuts and tax rises. The economy shrank by about 27% from 2008 to 2013, unemployment reached 27.8% and youth unemployment 59%. Debt as a share of GDP rose, because GDP fell faster than the debt did. The IMF later admitted it had underestimated how much cuts would hurt growth.
  • Greece left its last programme in 2018. After a pandemic peak of 209%, its debt has fallen to about 145% of GDP and it now runs a budget surplus. The crisis is still the clearest case of what happens when a country shares a currency it can't devalue.

Part 1Joining the euro club

At the end of the 1990s, Europe was preparing its boldest project since the end of the cold war: a single currency. No more swapping drachmas for marks or francs, no more exchange rates moving against exporters. To join, a country had to pass a set of tests, the Maastricht criteria: low inflation, low long-term interest rates, a stable exchange rate, a budget deficit below 3% of GDP, and government debt below 60% of GDP, or at least falling towards it2.

Eleven countries launched the euro in January 1999. Greece wasn't among them: its inflation and deficit were too high. It tried again, and joined on 1 January 2001, with debt still above 100% of GDP. For Greece, staying out meant higher borrowing costs and being left behind by the rest of Europe. Getting in meant cheap money and a seat at the top table.

Part 2Numbers that didn't add up

Two things later cast doubt on how Greece got in. The first was a set of deals with Goldman Sachs. In December 2000 and June 2001, Greece swapped some of its foreign-currency debt into euros at an exchange rate set off the market price. The effect, by Goldman's own account, was to cut Greece's reported debt by €2.4 billion, about 1.6% of GDP, in exchange for higher payments later3. Goldman says the trades were standard and within the reporting rules of the time; critics called them a loan dressed up as a currency trade. They were legal, and they weren't disclosed.

The second was the statistics themselves. In 2004, after a change of government, Eurostat audited Greece's figures and found that deficits had been under-reported for years, mostly through military spending and social-security accounts. On the revised numbers, the deficit had been above 3% in every year since 19974. The figure for 1999, the year Greece's entry was judged on, came out just over the line and was disputed. Greece was already in. Nothing much happened.

The warning nobody acted onThe 2004 audit was the first public sign that Greece's numbers could not be trusted. The European Commission began legal action over the misreporting, but Greece kept the euro, kept borrowing, and kept reporting figures that would later be revised again.

Part 3The cheap-money years

For most Greeks, the early euro years felt like a golden age. Investors treated Greek bonds as almost as safe as German ones, so the government, companies and households could borrow at rates Greece had never seen. Money flowed into Athens. Public-sector wages and pensions rose, the economy grew by 4% or more in most years, and in 2004 Athens hosted the Olympic Games. After decades as Europe's poorer cousin, Greece felt it had arrived.

Underneath, the numbers didn't improve. Even in the boom, Greece ran a deficit every single year, never smaller than 4% of GDP, and debt stayed above 100%. Tax collection was weak and the public sector large. Wages rose faster than productivity, so Greek goods and services became steadily more expensive than Germany's, and, inside the euro, there was no drachma to devalue to fix it. Then, in 2008, the global financial crisis hit. Tourism and shipping, two of Greece's biggest earners, slumped, and tax revenue fell with them.

Part 4The reveal

In October 2009 a new government under George Papandreou took office and opened the books. The previous government had told Brussels that the year's deficit would be 3.7% of GDP. The new one said it would be about 12.5%. The European Commission later reported "severe irregularities" in Greek statistics and found that the figures had been misreported more than once6. On today's revised data, the 2009 deficit was 15.4%, and debt was 128% of GDP.

Trust went in a matter of weeks. The credit-rating agencies cut Greece's rating again and again, down to junk. Interest rates on Greek bonds rose until the government could no longer afford to borrow from markets at all. By spring 2010 it was close to running out of cash to pay its bills and repay maturing debt.

A country that can't borrow, can't print money and can't devalue has only one place left to go: other governments.

Part 5Bailouts and austerity

A Greek default would have hit the European banks holding its bonds and could have spread to Portugal, Ireland, Spain and Italy. So in May 2010 the "troika", the European Commission, the European Central Bank and the IMF, agreed the first rescue: €110 billion of loans, €80 billion from euro-area governments and €30 billion from the IMF7. In return, Greece had to cut spending, raise taxes, cut pensions and public wages, and sell state assets.

It wasn't enough. In 2012 came a second programme of about €130 billion, and with it the largest write-off of privately held government debt ever arranged: private bondholders swapped their bonds for new ones worth 53.5% less on paper, cutting Greece's debt by about €107 billion8. You can see it in the chart: debt dips in 2012, then climbs again.

The reason it climbed again is the crux of the austerity argument. Debt is measured as a share of GDP. Cutting spending in a recession reduces borrowing, but it also shrinks the economy, and if GDP falls faster than the debt, the ratio rises. How much an economy shrinks for each euro of cuts is called the fiscal multiplier. In 2013 two IMF economists, Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh, found that forecasters had assumed multipliers of about 0.5 early in the crisis, when the real ones had been well above 19.

The human cost was enormous. The economy shrank by about a quarter from 2008 to 2013. Unemployment reached 27.8% in 2013, and nearly 60% for under-25s. Pensions were cut repeatedly, hospitals ran short of supplies, and hundreds of thousands of young Greeks left the country to find work. In 2013 the IMF's own review of its first Greek programme listed "notable failures": market confidence was not restored, banks lost 30% of their deposits, and the recession was far deeper than expected. It also said debt should have been restructured sooner10.

Part 62015: the showdown

By 2015 Greeks had lived through five years of cuts. In January they elected Syriza, a left-wing party led by Alexis Tsipras that promised to end austerity and renegotiate the debt. Months of talks with the other euro countries went nowhere. Savers, fearing that Greece would leave the euro and their deposits would be turned back into drachmas, pulled money out: Greek banks lost 27% of their deposits in the first half of the year, a slow-motion bank run.

At the end of June, Tsipras called a referendum on the creditors' terms. The banks closed, and when they reopened, capital controls limited cash withdrawals to €60 a day. On 30 June Greece failed to make a €1.5 billion payment to the IMF, falling into arrears, the first developed country to do so. On 5 July, 61% of voters rejected the creditors' terms11.

A week later, facing the prospect of being forced out of the euro, Tsipras accepted a third programme on tougher terms than the ones voters had rejected. The European Stability Mechanism lent up to €86 billion, of which Greece drew €61.9 billion, from August 2015 until the programme ended on 20 August 201812.

Part 7What went wrong

Greece's crisis had more than one cause, and people still argue about which mattered most. Four stand out.

At home

Spending it didn't have

Greece ran deficits through the best years it ever had, collected too little tax, and reported numbers that turned out to be wrong. Cheap euro borrowing let that go on far longer than it could have with the drachma.

The design

A currency it couldn't adjust

A country with its own currency can let it fall, making its exports cheaper, and its central bank can print money to pay debts. In the euro, Greece could do neither, so the only way to become competitive was to cut wages and prices: a slow, painful "internal devaluation".

The cure

Austerity in a slump

Critics say cuts in a deep recession made the debt ratio worse, as the IMF's own research suggests. Defenders reply that Greece had a 15% deficit and no lenders: someone had to fund it, and creditors' taxpayers would only do so with conditions. Both can be true.

The fourth is who the rescues rescued. Critics point out that most of the bailout money went to repaying Greece's existing creditors, including European banks, and its interest bill, rather than into the Greek economy. Supporters of the programmes say that was the point: to stop a Greek default from spreading through Europe's banks. Critics say Greek citizens paid for a crisis that their creditors had helped to cause.

Greece has since recovered further than many expected. Growth returned, unemployment has fallen below 10%, and the budget has been in surplus. Debt jumped to 209% of GDP in the pandemic and has fallen to about 145% since, helped by long, cheap loans from the euro area. But the economy is still smaller than it was in 2008, and a generation lost its best years of work. The lesson most often drawn is the simplest: a currency union needs honest numbers to join, and a way to help members in trouble that doesn't depend on crushing them.
