Who Gets the Biggest Slice?

“The market rose 1% today” depends on which market you mean. The S&P 500, the Dow and an equal-weight fund can hold the same companies in very different amounts, because each uses a different rule for deciding how much each company counts. Here are the three rules, what they do to your money, and why a stock split can change a company's place in the Dow.

Key findings

  • An index is a list of companies plus a rule for how much each one counts. The three common rules are market-cap weighting (by company value), equal weighting (every company the same) and price weighting (by the price of one share).
  • Most index funds, including those tracking the S&P 500, MSCI World and Singapore's STI, weight by market value. That makes them cheap to run and self-adjusting, but top-heavy: the ten largest S&P 500 companies passed 40%1 of the index in 2025, a level S&P says was last seen in the mid-1960s.
  • An equal-weight S&P 500 fund puts about 0.2%2 in each company at every quarterly rebalance, which means regularly selling what has risen and buying what has fallen.
  • The Dow and Japan's Nikkei 225 weight by share price, so a $600 share counts ten times as much as a $60 one regardless of company size. Uniqlo's owner, Fast Retailing, passed 10% of the Nikkei, and a stock split can shrink a company's influence overnight without changing its value.

Part 1An index is a recipe

A stock index is two decisions written down. The first is which companies are in it: the 500 large US companies a committee picks for the S&P 500, the 30 biggest on the Singapore Exchange for the Straits Times Index, the 225 Tokyo blue chips in the Nikkei. The second is how much each one counts. That second decision gets far less attention, and it can matter as much as the first.

For most of their history indexes were just a number in the newspaper, a way to say whether the market was up or down. Since index funds and ETFs arrived, the recipe has become an instruction. A fund that tracks an index buys each company in the proportion the index says, so the weighting rule decides how your money is actually spread. Two funds holding exactly the same 500 companies can still own very different portfolios.

Part 2By company value

The most common rule weights each company by its market capitalisation, the share price multiplied by the number of shares. A company worth $3 trillion counts a hundred times as much as one worth $30 billion. The S&P 500, the Nasdaq-100, MSCI's world and emerging-market indexes, and Singapore's Straits Times Index all work this way. Most use free float: they count only the shares available to ordinary investors, leaving out large blocks held by founders, governments or parent companies.

The appeal is that the index mirrors the market itself. If you added up every share of every company in it, you would own exactly these proportions, so a cap-weighted fund holds “the market” as it is. It also looks after itself. When a company's price rises, its weight rises by the same amount, so the fund needs no trading to stay in line. Shares only need buying or selling when companies join, leave, or issue or retire shares. That is a large part of why these funds are so cheap to run.

The cost is concentration. Because weight follows value, the winners take up more and more of the index. In 2025 the ten largest S&P 500 companies passed 40% of its value. S&P Dow Jones Indices said that level had not been seen since the mid-1960s; around 2015 the top ten were under a fifth. Much of that weight sits in a handful of technology companies whose fortunes are tied to the same trend, so an investor who thinks they own “500 companies” is, in practice, making a large bet on a few.

Index providers have added limits for indexes where one company can dominate. Many sector and country indexes cap any single company, often at 10% to 25%, and redistribute the excess. A broad S&P 500 fund has no such cap, which is why its exposure to its largest names keeps rising as they grow.

Part 3Every company equal

The simplest alternative ignores size. An equal-weight index gives every member the same slice. The S&P 500 Equal Weight Index, tracked by an Invesco ETF launched in 2003, sets each company to about 0.2% (one in 500) at every quarterly rebalance. A company worth $20 billion counts for exactly as much as one worth $3 trillion.

That changes what you own. Compared with the regular S&P 500, the equal-weight version holds far less of the giants and far more of the smaller members, so it leans toward mid-sized companies and toward sectors such as industrials and utilities that have many members but few giants. When the largest companies lead the market, as they have for much of the past decade, equal weight lags. When the rest of the market catches up, it can lead.

It also has to trade. Between rebalances, winners grow above 0.2% and losers shrink below it. Each quarter the fund sells some of what has risen and buys more of what has fallen, to bring everything back to equal. That is a built-in “sell high, buy low” habit, which can help when prices swing back and hurt when a strong trend keeps running. The trading costs money too, so equal-weight funds usually charge more than the cheapest cap-weighted funds.

Equal weight is not the same as “safer”Spreading money evenly removes the dependence on a few giants, but it adds weight to smaller companies, which tend to swing more on their own. Which version does better depends on which part of the market leads, and that changes over the years.

Part 4By share price

The oldest rule is the strangest. A price-weighted index adds up the price of one share of each company and divides by a number called the divisor. Charles Dow did this in 1896 because it was easy to work out by hand, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average still works this way, as does Japan's Nikkei 225.

The result is that a company's weight depends on its share price, not its size. In 2025 Goldman Sachs, with a share price4 above $600, was the heaviest company in the Dow at roughly 9–10%, while Nvidia, then one of the most valuable companies in the world but with shares around $100–200 after a split, made up about 2%. Because the Dow's divisor is around 0.163, every $1 move in any member's share price moves the index by about six points, whether the company is worth $50 billion or $4 trillion.

Japan shows the extreme. Fast Retailing, the owner of Uniqlo6, has one of the highest share prices on the Tokyo exchange, and its weight in the Nikkei 225 passed 10%. In 2022 Nikkei began capping how much any one company could count, and Fast Retailing was the first company the cap applied to.

Price weighting also produces a quirk ordinary investors notice: a stock split. When a company splits each share into four, its value does not change, but its share price falls to a quarter, and so does its weight in a price-weighted index. Apple's 4-for-1 split in 2020 took it from the heaviest stock in the Dow to well down the list. Nvidia's 10-for-1 split in 2024 brought its share price down to a level where adding it would not let one stock dominate, and it replaced Intel in the Dow5 that November. Amazon, after a 20-for-1 split in 2022, had joined earlier that year. Toggle the split in the figure above to see the effect.

Part 5The same day, three answers

Because the rules differ, indexes built from overlapping companies can give quite different readings on the same day. A strong day for a few technology giants can lift the S&P 500 while the equal-weight version barely moves. A big move in one high-priced stock such as Goldman Sachs can swing the Dow by hundreds of points while hardly registering in the S&P 500. Headlines that say “the market” usually mean whichever index the writer looked at.

For an investor the practical question is what each fund concentrates in. A cap-weighted world or S&P 500 fund is the cheapest and most common choice, and its weight in the largest companies is the price of that simplicity. An equal-weight fund spreads the bet more evenly at a higher cost and with more trading. A price-weighted index is mostly a historical habit: hardly anyone would design one today, but the Dow's long history keeps it in the headlines. Part 6Price times shares

Market value is share price × number of shares. Index weighting is usually discussed as if only the price moves. But companies can change the other half of the sum. When a company issues new shares, there are more slices of the same pie. When it buys back its own shares and cancels them, there are fewer, and each remaining share owns a bigger piece of the company.

Those buybacks have become one of the largest sources of demand for US shares, alongside index funds themselves: S&P 500 companies spent a record $942.5 billion7 buying back their own stock in 2024. How that buying works, who sells to the company, and why it can be paused at any time are covered in the companion piece, Shrinking the Pie: How Stock Buybacks Work.
