The Lord's Cut

Make a dollar for a few cents, then spend it at face value, and you keep the difference. That gap is seigniorage, the oldest profit in finance. Medieval lords took it by minting coins, and took more by quietly shaving the silver out of them. Today a government, a bank and a stablecoin issuer each take their own version of it. Here is how the same trick has run from the mint to the blockchain.

Key findings

  • Seigniorage is the profit an issuer makes from putting money into circulation: the face value of the money minus the cost of creating it. The word comes from the Old French1 for a feudal lord's right to mint coins.
  • Early mints charged a fee to coin your metal. Rulers took a bigger, hidden cut by debasement, lowering the precious-metal content while keeping the face value. The Roman denarius fell from about 98% silver2 to roughly 2% over three centuries. Small coins now run the opposite way: a US penny costs more to make4 than it's worth, a loss called reverse seigniorage.
  • Paper and digital money make the gap enormous: a $1006 note costs about nine cents to print, so almost the whole $100 is seigniorage. A modern central bank collects it as interest on the bonds it buys with new money and hands the profit to the treasury. Because about half of all US dollar notes are held abroad, much of that profit comes from foreigners.
  • The same logic now runs through bank deposits, stablecoins (take a dollar, hold Treasuries, keep the yield) and proposed central-bank digital currencies. In every case the issuer profits from money it created cheaply, and over-issuing it causes inflation, which is the natural limit. Zimbabwe found that limit in 2008, when prices rose by about 80 billion percent in a single month.

Part 1What seigniorage is

Here is the whole idea in one line. If you can create money for less than it's worth and spend it at full value, you pocket the difference. That difference is seigniorage. A $100 bill costs about nine cents to print, so on paper its issuer is $99.91 ahead the moment the note goes out into the economy. (Part 4 shows how a modern central bank actually collects that gap.)

The name is medieval. It comes from the Old French seigneuriage, the right of the seigneur, the lord, to mint money, and it has meant "the profit from issuing money" ever since. The thing being issued has changed many times, from silver coins to paper notes to numbers in a database, but the profit has the same shape every time: face value minus cost to create.

Part 2The mint and the shave

For most of history money was the metal. A silver coin was worth roughly the silver in it. So where was the profit? Two places.

First, an honest fee. If you brought raw silver to the ruler's mint to be struck into coins, the mint kept a small cut for the service. That fee was the original seigniorage, modest and openly charged.

Second, the shave. A ruler who controlled the mint could debase the coinage: issue a coin that still said "one denarius" but contained less silver than before, melt in cheap base metal, and spend the extra coins at full value. It's seigniorage by stealth, and rulers short of money reached for it again and again. Rome did it for centuries.

Part 3When it runs in reverse

Seigniorage isn't always a profit. When the cost of making a coin meets or beats its face value, the issuer earns little, nothing, or even takes a loss. That's negative, or reverse, seigniorage, and it shows up in two very different places: at the honest top of the market, and at the humble bottom of it.

At the top sit bullion coins, the opposite of a debased one. A coin like the American Gold Eagle carries a $505 face value but contains a full ounce of gold worth thousands of dollars. The face value is almost symbolic; the coin is worth its metal. Governments don't issue these to pocket a gap, because there isn't one. The mint sells them at the gold price plus a small premium that just covers striking and handling, so the buyer pays for the metal and the making. The whole point is that the metal is real, which is also why there's no seigniorage profit to take.

At the bottom sit the smallest everyday coins, where the metal and manufacturing now cost more than the coin is worth. Here the issuer loses money on every piece it strikes. It's the mirror image of Roman debasement: instead of the coin being worth less than its stamp, the coin costs more than its stamp.

Everyday silver coins went through the same squeeze, and in the US it ended with silver being dropped from them. Until 1965 dimes, quarters and half-dollars were 90% silver. At about $1.29 an ounce15, the silver in a coin was worth exactly its face value. For years the Treasury kept the price well below that by selling from its own stockpile at around 91 cents, but in the early 1960s demand, much of it from industry, outran the stockpile and pushed the price up to the line. People started pulling silver coins out of circulation, and in 1964 a coin shortage spread across the country while the Mint struck as fast as it could.

The answer was the Coinage Act of 1965: dimes and quarters became copper cores clad in copper-nickel, with no silver at all, and the half-dollar dropped to 40% silver until 1970. As Gresham’s law predicts, the old coins vanished into drawers and melting pots while the new ones circulated. Today a pre-1965 quarter holds about 0.18 ounces of silver, worth more than $5 at any silver price above $30, which it has been since 2024. Its face value is still 25 cents.

Why the humble coin lost its profitA penny that cost a fraction of a cent to make a century ago now costs several cents, because metal and manufacturing got more expensive while the face value stayed frozen at one cent. The same pressure explains the whole arc of this story: as coins became loss-makers, seigniorage migrated to where production is almost free, first to paper notes, then to digital money. The profit didn't disappear; it moved to a cheaper medium.

Part 4Paper money

Paper breaks the link between a coin and its metal, and that's exactly what makes the profit explode. A banknote is worth its face value by law and habit, not by the paper. So the cost of creating it falls to almost nothing while the face value stays whole.

But it took two centuries for paper to come fully loose. For most of that time a note was a promise of metal: hand it in and the issuer owed you gold or silver. That promise was a leash. An issuer could print only as many notes as it could plausibly redeem, so its seigniorage was capped by the metal in its vault. The story of modern money is that leash being let out, then cut.

Bretton Woods carried a flaw that an economist, Robert Triffin, pointed out to Congress in 1960. The world needed ever more dollars to use as reserves, and the only way to supply them was for the US to run deficits, sending more dollars abroad than came back. But the more dollars piled up overseas, the less believable the promise to swap each one for gold. Either the world ran short of dollars, or the dollar ran short of credibility. This is the Triffin dilemma, and by 1971 foreign-held dollars far exceeded the US gold stock.

Cutting the link in 1971 didn’t end the dollar’s special role; it removed the brake on it. Every currency became fiat, money by decree, worth what its issuer’s trustworthiness says it is. That is why a $100 note can now cost a few cents to make with no gold behind it at all. Here is how that profit works today.

In the United States, the Treasury's Bureau of Engraving and Printing makes the notes and the Federal Reserve issues them. Printing a $100 note costs only around nine cents; the other $99.91 is seigniorage. But a central bank doesn't simply spend new money. It buys assets with it, mostly government bonds, and those assets pay interest. That interest, minus the Fed's running costs, is the modern form of seigniorage.

So there are two ways to count it. The accounting way is the one in Part 1: face value minus printing cost, nearly $100 per $100 note. The economic way is the interest earned on what the note bought: hold a $100 note in your wallet for a year at 4% interest rates and the Fed collects about $4 on the bond behind it, every year the note stays out. Over many years the two meet, but the second is how the money actually arrives. Cash is, in effect, an interest-free loan from everyone who holds it to the central bank.

And many holders aren't American. Federal Reserve staff estimate that about 45% of all US banknotes, and around two-thirds of $100 notes, are held abroad, as savings7 and for trade in countries where the local currency is less trusted. That's roughly $950 billion of interest-free lending from the rest of the world, a quiet part of what is sometimes called the dollar's "exorbitant privilege."

Where the profit goesThe Federal Reserve isn't allowed to keep its profit. After expenses, it remits the surplus to the US Treasury, so the seigniorage ends up funding the government. In 2021 that came to about $109 billion8, and in 2022 about $76 billion. Then the payments stopped.

The reason is the other side of the Fed's balance sheet. Since 2008 it has bought far more bonds than there is cash to back them, paying for the rest with bank reserves, which the Fed pays interest on. When rates were near zero that cost nothing. When rates jumped in 2022, the interest the Fed paid on reserves overtook what its older, low-yielding bonds earned. Try it below.

Notice what never loses money: the cash. Notes cost the Fed no interest, so the bonds behind them always earn. The losses come from the reserves, which are borrowed money with a price. That's the line between seigniorage and ordinary banking, drawn in the Fed's own accounts.

Part 5Bank money

Most money today isn't cash at all. It's the balance in your bank account, created when banks make loans. Commercial banks don't print notes, so their version of the profit is different, but it rhymes with seigniorage.

When a bank lends, it credits the borrower's account with new deposit money, backed by the loan it now holds as an asset. The bank's profit is the interest it charges on that lending, over what it pays to fund itself. It is earning a return on money that largely came into being with a keystroke. Economists debate whether to call bank profit true seigniorage, but the family resemblance is clear: create the medium cheaply, earn on it at full value.

Part 6Stablecoins

The newest issuers look surprisingly like the oldest. A stablecoin such as USDT promises to be worth one dollar (see our explainer on stablecoins). Here is how the issuer profits from making it.

The catch, as with any issuer, is the promise to redeem. The dollar you handed over is now tied up in a bond; if too many holders ask for their dollars back at once and the issuer can't sell the backing fast enough, the peg can break. That risk is the price of the profit.

Notice who doesn't get the interest: you. In the US that is now the law. The GENIUS10 Act, signed in July 2025, bars stablecoin issuers from paying interest or yield to holders, so the issuer keeps the whole return on the backing, just as a central bank pays nothing on cash. The fight has moved to the middlemen. Circle, which issues USDC, shares much of its interest income with partners that distribute the coin; it paid Coinbase about $908 million in 2024, and Coinbase passes some of that to its users as "rewards." Banks argue that is interest by another name. Who captures the seigniorage, the issuer, the exchange or the user, is now a live legal question.

Part 7Digital central-bank money

Several central banks are exploring a central-bank digital currency (CBDC)11: official money issued as digital tokens rather than paper. For seigniorage, a CBDC works like physical cash, with one difference. There's no paper or ink, so the cost of issuing falls to almost zero. The same face-value-minus-cost profit stays with the central bank, and the same assets sit behind it.

The bigger effect is who earns it. If people moved savings out of bank deposits and into central-bank digital money, the profit that banks now make on deposits (Part 5) would move to the central bank, and banks would have less cheap funding to lend. That worry, along with privacy and control, is why many proposals cap how much CBDC one person can hold. The US has gone the other way: in January 2025 an executive order barred federal agencies from working to create one. Either way, it's a clean illustration of the trend across this whole story: as money grows more abstract, the cost of making it keeps falling toward zero, and the profit in issuing it keeps growing.

Part 8The same cut, and its limit

Line the eras up and the pattern is one idea wearing different clothes.

There is a hard limit, and it's the same one the Roman emperors hit. Seigniorage is only profitable while the money holds its value. Create too much of it and you flood the economy with claims on the same goods, and prices rise: that's inflation. A debased coin and an over-printed currency fail the same way, by making each unit worth less than before. Economists call this the inflation tax: the issuer gains, and everyone holding the money loses a slice of its value, without a vote or a bill. Push it to an extreme and you get hyperinflation. Zimbabwe printed money to pay its bills until, in November 2008, prices were rising by an estimated 79.6 billion12 percent a month, doubling roughly every day; the government issued a 100-trillion-dollar note, and soon after people abandoned the currency for US dollars. At that point the seigniorage is gone too, because nobody will hold the money long enough to be taxed by it.

The limit has a quieter form than hyperinflation: holders can simply leave. If they doubt an issuer, they can move their savings into money that nobody issues, which pays no cut to anyone. The oldest such money is gold, and central banks themselves have turned to it. They bought more than 1,00016 tonnes a year in 2022, 2023 and 2024, by the World Gold Council’s count, about twice the pace of the previous decade, with China, Poland, Turkey and India among the biggest buyers. They buy gold, not silver. Silver is worth roughly a hundredth as much as gold by weight, so the same value takes far more vault space, and with more than half its demand coming from industry, including solar panels, its price swings with the economy. Russia’s finance ministry said in 2024 it would add silver to the state’s precious-metals fund, a rare exception, and not a central-bank purchase. By 2024, at market prices, gold had overtaken the euro as the world’s second-largest reserve asset after the dollar, according to the European Central Bank.

Part of the reason is sanctions. When Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, Western governments froze about $300 billion of its central bank’s reserves held as dollars, euros and other currencies abroad. Gold in your own vault can’t be frozen with a keystroke. It isn’t untouchable, though: Venezuela has spent years unable to retrieve gold it stored at the Bank of England. The lesson central banks drew is narrower than “gold can’t be sanctioned”: gold you hold at home is the one reserve no foreign issuer can switch off. For the dollar, every tonne bought that way is a small vote of less trust, and a little less of the world’s savings lent to the US interest-free.

Seigniorage is the quiet profit in being the one who makes the money. Its only enemy is making too much of it.
