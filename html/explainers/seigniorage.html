The Lord's Cut

Make a dollar for a few cents, then spend it at face value, and you keep the difference. That gap is seigniorage, the oldest profit in finance. Medieval lords took it by minting coins, and took more by quietly shaving the silver out of them. Today a government, a bank and a stablecoin issuer each take their own version of it. Here is how the same trick has run from the mint to the blockchain.

Key findings

  • Seigniorage is the profit an issuer makes from putting money into circulation: the face value of the money minus the cost of creating it. The word comes from the Old French for a feudal lord's right to mint coins.
  • Early mints charged a fee to coin your metal. Rulers took a bigger, hidden cut by debasement, lowering the precious-metal content while keeping the face value. The Roman denarius fell from about 98% silver to roughly 2% over three centuries. Small coins now run the opposite way: a US penny costs more to make than it's worth, a loss called reverse seigniorage.
  • Paper and digital money make the gap enormous: a $100 note costs about nine cents to print, so almost the whole $100 is seigniorage. A modern central bank earns it as interest on the assets it buys with new money, and hands the profit to the treasury.
  • The same logic now runs through bank deposits, stablecoins (take a dollar, hold Treasuries, keep the yield) and proposed central-bank digital currencies. In every case the issuer profits from money it created cheaply, and over-issuing it causes inflation, which is the natural limit.

Part 1What seigniorage is

Here is the whole idea in one line. If you can create money for less than it's worth and spend it at full value, you pocket the difference. That difference is seigniorage. A $100 bill that costs about nine cents to print hands its issuer roughly $99.91 of pure profit the moment it's spent into the economy.

The name is medieval. It comes from the Old French seigneuriage, the right of the seigneur, the lord, to mint money, and it has meant "the profit from issuing money" ever since. The thing being issued has changed many times, from silver coins to paper notes to numbers in a database, but the profit has the same shape every time: face value minus cost to create.

Part 2The mint and the shave

For most of history money was the metal. A silver coin was worth roughly the silver in it. So where was the profit? Two places.

First, an honest fee. If you brought raw silver to the ruler's mint to be struck into coins, the mint kept a small cut for the service. That fee was the original seigniorage, modest and openly charged.

Second, the shave. A ruler who controlled the mint could debase the coinage: issue a coin that still said "one denarius" but contained less silver than before, melt in cheap base metal, and spend the extra coins at full value. It's seigniorage by stealth, and rulers short of money reached for it again and again. Rome did it for centuries.

Good money, bad moneyWhen a debased coin and a full-silver one both count as "one denarius," people spend the bad one and keep the good one. The pure coins disappear into hoards and only the watered-down ones circulate. That tendency, later summed up as "bad money drives out good," is Gresham's law, and it's the recurring cost of pushing seigniorage too hard.

Part 3When it runs in reverse

Seigniorage isn't always a profit. When the cost of making a coin meets or beats its face value, the issuer earns little, nothing, or even takes a loss. That's negative, or reverse, seigniorage, and it shows up in two very different places: at the honest top of the market, and at the humble bottom of it.

At the top sit bullion coins, the opposite of a debased one. A coin like the American Gold Eagle carries a $50 face value but contains a full ounce of gold worth thousands of dollars. The face value is almost symbolic; the coin is worth its metal. Governments don't issue these to pocket a gap, because there isn't one. The mint sells them at the gold price plus a small premium that just covers striking and handling, so the buyer pays for the metal and the making. The whole point is that the metal is real, which is also why there's no seigniorage profit to take.

At the bottom sit the smallest everyday coins, where the metal and manufacturing now cost more than the coin is worth. Here the issuer loses money on every piece it strikes. It's the mirror image of Roman debasement: instead of the coin being worth less than its stamp, the coin costs more than its stamp.

Why the humble coin lost its profitA penny that cost a fraction of a cent to make a century ago now costs several cents, because metal and manufacturing got more expensive while the face value stayed frozen at one cent. The same pressure explains the whole arc of this story: as coins became loss-makers, seigniorage migrated to where production is almost free, first to paper notes, then to digital money. The profit didn't disappear; it moved to a cheaper medium.

Part 4Paper money

Paper breaks the link between a coin and its metal, and that's exactly what makes the profit explode. A banknote is worth its face value by law and habit, not by the paper. So the cost of creating it falls to almost nothing while the face value stays whole.

In the United States, the Treasury's Bureau of Engraving and Printing makes the notes and the Federal Reserve issues them. Printing a $100 note costs only around nine cents; the other $99.91 is seigniorage. But a central bank doesn't simply spend new money. It buys assets with it, mostly government bonds, and those assets pay interest. That interest, minus the Fed's running costs, is the modern form of seigniorage.

Where the profit goesThe Federal Reserve isn't allowed to keep its profit. After expenses, it remits the surplus to the US Treasury, so the seigniorage ends up funding the government. In 2021 that remittance was about $76 billion. (When interest rates jumped from 2022, the Fed's own interest costs rose above its income and the remittances stopped for a time, a reminder that seigniorage isn't guaranteed.)

Part 5Bank money

Most money today isn't cash at all. It's the balance in your bank account, created when banks make loans. Commercial banks don't print notes, so their version of the profit is different, but it rhymes with seigniorage.

When a bank lends, it credits the borrower's account with new deposit money, backed by the loan it now holds as an asset. The bank's profit is the interest it charges on that lending, over what it pays to fund itself. It is earning a return on money that largely came into being with a keystroke. Economists debate whether to call bank profit true seigniorage, but the family resemblance is clear: create the medium cheaply, earn on it at full value.

Part 6Stablecoins

The newest issuers look surprisingly like the oldest. A stablecoin such as USDT promises to be worth one dollar (see our explainer on stablecoins). Here is how the issuer profits from making it.

The catch, as with any issuer, is the promise to redeem. The dollar you handed over is now tied up in a bond; if too many holders ask for their dollars back at once and the issuer can't sell the backing fast enough, the peg can break. That risk is the price of the profit.

Part 7Digital central-bank money

Several central banks are exploring a central-bank digital currency (CBDC): official money issued as digital tokens rather than paper. For seigniorage, a CBDC works like physical cash, with one difference. There's no paper or ink, so the cost of issuing falls to almost zero. The same face-value-minus-cost profit stays with the central bank, and the same assets sit behind it.

The arguments around CBDCs are mostly about control, privacy and the role of banks, not the seigniorage, which looks much like it does for cash today. But it's a clean illustration of the trend across this whole story: as money grows more abstract, the cost of making it keeps falling toward zero, and the profit in issuing it keeps growing.

Part 8The same cut, and its limit

Line the eras up and the pattern is one idea wearing different clothes.

There is a hard limit, and it's the same one the Roman emperors hit. Seigniorage is only profitable while the money holds its value. Create too much of it and you flood the economy with claims on the same goods, and prices rise: that's inflation. A debased coin and an over-printed currency fail the same way, by making each unit worth less than before. Push it to an extreme and you get the hyperinflations where money becomes nearly worthless.

Seigniorage is the quiet profit in being the one who makes the money. Its only enemy is making too much of it.

So whenever something new claims to be money, ask two things.

QUESTION 1

Where exactly does the issuer's profit come from?

QUESTION 2

What stops the issuer from just making unlimited money?
