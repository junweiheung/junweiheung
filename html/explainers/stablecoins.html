Keeping a Coin Worth a Dollar

A stablecoin promises one thing: that it will always be worth a dollar. Some keep that promise with cash in a bank, some with crypto locked in a contract, and some with nothing but code and confidence. The last kind once wiped out about $40 billion in three days. Here's what keeps a dollar a dollar, and what breaks it.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics

This explains how stablecoins try to stay worth a dollar and why some fail, plus a few related crypto instruments. Mechanisms are described in plain terms; specific products (USDT, USDC, BOLD, TerraUSD, STRC) change their terms, reserves and rates over time, so check each issuer's current disclosures before relying on anything here. Where I describe risks, I am setting out how a design can fail, not predicting that a named product will. This is educational, not investment advice.

Key findings

  • A stablecoin is a crypto token that tries to track a reference value, almost always one US dollar. How it holds that peg is the whole story.
  • Fiat-backed coins (USDT, USDC) hold cash and reserves 1:1 and lean on trust in the issuer and audits. Crypto-backed coins (BOLD) lock more crypto than they issue. Algorithmic coins use code alone.
  • Algorithmic designs are the most capital-efficient and the most fragile. TerraUSD and its sister token LUNA collapsed together in a "death spiral" in May 2022, erasing roughly $40 billion.
  • Even sound-looking designs can break: a crypto-backed coin can become underbacked if its collateral crashes faster than the system can sell it.

Part 1What a stablecoin is

Cryptocurrencies run on a blockchain: a shared ledger no single company controls. Most crypto prices swing wildly, which makes them hard to use as money. A stablecoin is the fix: a token designed to track a steady reference value, almost always one US dollar. Type "1 USDC" and it should be worth a dollar today, tomorrow, and next year.

The promise is easy to make and hard to keep. A stablecoin's price is still set by trading: if enough holders sell and too few buy, the price can slip below a dollar, because the market clears at whatever price a seller will accept. What stops that slip, or fails to, is the coin's design. There are three main designs.

Part 2Three ways to hold a dollar

Every stablecoin answers one question: what actually stands behind the dollar it promises?

Part 3Fiat-backed: trust the issuer

The simplest design. For every token in circulation, the issuer claims to hold one dollar of cash or safe reserves, often including US Treasuries. USDT (Tether) and USDC (Circle) work this way. Hand a token back and you should get a dollar.

Because you can't see the bank account, this design runs on trust, propped up three ways: issuers publish periodic audits or attestations to show the reserves exist; they keep policies to restrict or delay redemptions if too many people cash out at once; and the market itself arbitrages small gaps. Still, the price is set by trading, so a fiat-backed coin can briefly slip under a dollar when sellers pile up faster than buyers, which is exactly what a bank run looks like.

Part 4Crypto-backed: over-collateralise

Here there's no bank to trust. Instead you lock more crypto than the stablecoin you mint, and everyone can see the collateral on-chain. BOLD is a crypto-backed stablecoin that targets $1 and is backed by ETH. You lock ETH in a smart contract, borrow freshly created BOLD against it, and if your collateral falls too far, the system liquidates it to cover the debt.

A worked exampleLock about $2,000 of ETH and you can borrow roughly $1,000 of BOLD. Spend that BOLD as dollars, then later repay the $1,000 of BOLD plus interest to unlock your ETH. You've borrowed against your crypto without selling it. The extra collateral, the "over" in over-collateralised, is the safety buffer.

That buffer is the whole defence, and it can fail. The danger isn't an ETH price drop by itself; it's a drop so fast that the system can't sell the collateral in time.

Part 5Algorithmic: the Terra spiral

The most capital-efficient design holds almost no real collateral. It keeps the peg with code and a second token. TerraUSD (UST) was the famous example: each UST could always be swapped for $1 worth of LUNA, a companion token whose value rested on how useful the Terra network was. In calm markets, that swap kept UST at a dollar. Under stress, it became a trap.

  1. UST slips below $1. Selling pressure pushes it under the peg.
  2. Holders swap UST for LUNA. Each gets $1 worth of newly made LUNA and sells it, pushing LUNA's price down.
  3. Terra prints more LUNA. To give the next person $1 of LUNA at a lower price, it must mint even more, driving the price down again.
  4. Confidence breaks. Terra tried to save one falling token by printing another. Buyers fled, and both UST and LUNA collapsed together.

Terra tried to save a falling token by printing another one. The market lost faith in both.

Part 6Bitcoin, Ether and a Bitcoin-backed stock

Stablecoins live on top of two networks worth knowing.

Bitcoin was the first widely successful cryptocurrency: scarce digital money with no central operator, which is why people call it "digital gold." Its coin is BTC. Ethereum is a platform where developers build things on top: stablecoins, lending, NFTs and other apps. Its coin is Ether (ETH), the same ETH that backs BOLD above.

Bitcoin's rise has also spawned instruments built around holding it. One is STRC, a perpetual preferred share issued by Strategy (formerly MicroStrategy), which uses the proceeds to buy and hold Bitcoin.

Why critics are wary of STRCThe attraction is obvious: a high, bond-like yield. The worry is where the yield comes from. Critics argue the structure leans on new investors' money and on Bitcoin continuing to rise to cover the dividend, needing at least a couple of percent of Bitcoin appreciation a year just to break even on the payout. Paying dividends largely out of fresh capital is the hallmark of a Ponzi-like structure, and sceptics call the model unsustainable on those grounds. Whether that criticism proves right depends on Bitcoin's price and Strategy's own disclosures, which you should read directly.

The thread through all of it is the same question a stablecoin asks: what actually stands behind the promise? Cash you must trust, crypto you can see, code you have to believe in, or a rising asset that has to keep rising.
