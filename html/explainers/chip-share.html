Three Companies, One Industry

Intel once owned the processor outright. Today the fight between AMD, Intel and Nvidia splits across separate markets, server and PC chips, AI accelerators, gaming graphics, and embedded, and in each of them the leader is a different company. Here is who holds what, and how the lead changed hands.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics
November 24, 2026 · 10 min read

In this story

A market-share snapshot of three US chip companies, segment by segment, as of the third quarter of 2026, with historical context. Current figures come from Mercury Research (x86 CPUs), Jon Peddie Research (graphics) and industry estimates (AI accelerators, FPGAs) as summarised in my notes; shares move every quarter and different trackers count differently (units vs revenue, discrete vs all graphics), so treat them as approximate. This explains a market; it isn't investment advice.

Key findings

  • There is no single "chip market." AMD, Intel and Nvidia lead in different places: Intel still ships the most PC CPUs, AMD leads cloud server CPUs, owns consoles and leads FPGAs, and Nvidia dominates AI and gaming graphics.
  • In x86 server CPUs, AMD hit a record 35.5% of units in Q3 2026 (up from 27.8% a year earlier), and 46.8% of mainstream cloud/enterprise servers, a level it last approached in the Opteron days of 2006.
  • In AI accelerators, Nvidia holds roughly 75–81% of revenue; AMD about 5–7%, with custom hyperscaler chips (Google TPU, Broadcom-built) around 12–15%.
  • In discrete gaming GPUs, Nvidia holds about 90% of shipments. But AMD powers essentially all current-generation consoles and most PC handhelds.

Part 1How we got here

For most of the x86 era, there was one giant and one gadfly. Intel, founded in 1968, set the pace; AMD lived in its shadow, with one brief exception. In 2006 AMD's Opteron server chips reached about a quarter of the x86 server market, its high-water mark. Then a weak chip design (Bulldozer) and years of losses pushed its server share toward the low single digits, and by the mid-2010s Intel controlled roughly 90% or more of server CPUs.

The comeback came with the Zen architecture and the EPYC server line from 2017. AMD broke back through 10% server share in 2021 and kept climbing. Meanwhile Nvidia, long the gaming-graphics leader, turned its GPUs into the default engine of the AI boom, and AMD bought its way into embedded chips by acquiring Xilinx in 2022. The result is today's split market.

Part 2Data centre: two very different races

The data centre is really two markets. In server CPUs, AMD's EPYC has stormed back: 35.5% of all x86 server units in Q3 2026, up from 27.8% a year earlier, and 46.8% once you narrow to mainstream cloud and enterprise two-socket servers, almost half. Intel's Xeon still holds the majority overall, but the gap is the narrowest in decades.

In AI accelerators, the picture flips entirely. This is a $200 billion-plus market, and Nvidia owns roughly 75–81% of the revenue. AMD's Instinct line holds about 5–7% (and may climb toward 7–10% as its MI450 and Helios racks ship), while custom hyperscaler chips, Google's TPU and Broadcom-built accelerators, take around 12–15%. Intel is a rounding error here.

Part 3Client PCs: Intel still ships the most

In the PCs people actually buy, Intel remains ahead by units. In Q3 2026 AMD held 29.9% of x86 desktop CPUs (down from 34.9% the prior quarter, as GPU supply bottlenecks hit gaming-PC builds) against Intel's 70.1%, and a record 29.2% of laptop processors against Intel's 70.8%. Combine client and server and AMD sits at 30.1% of all x86 units, up from 25.6% a year earlier, its highest ever, while Intel's share dipped below 70% for the first time since 1995.

Part 4Gaming: it depends how you count

Gaming graphics is the segment where the headline number misleads most. In discrete desktop graphics cards, Nvidia holds about 90% of shipments, AMD about 8%, and Intel about 2%. But count integrated graphics too, the graphics built into Ryzen and Intel processors, and it reshuffles: in the second quarter of 2026, Intel had about 56%, Nvidia about 23% and AMD about 21%. Intel leads simply because most PCs, especially cheap laptops, have no separate graphics card at all. Its share has slipped from 71% at the end of 2022 as Ryzen chips with built-in Radeon graphics sell more.

And there's a market Nvidia isn't in at all. AMD's semi-custom chips power essentially 100% of current-generation consoles, the PlayStation 5 and PS5 Pro and the Xbox Series X and S, with only Nintendo's Switch using a different design. AMD also powers most Windows and SteamOS handhelds, the Steam Deck, ROG Ally and Legion Go. Nvidia's near-monopoly on discrete cards sits next to AMD's near-monopoly on consoles.

Part 5Embedded: the quiet lead

The least-watched segment is one AMD bought its way into. With Xilinx, AMD holds more than half of the FPGA market, the reconfigurable chips used in telecoms, aerospace, industrial and defence gear, according to Yole Group. That lead is old: back in 2010, Xilinx already had about 52% to Altera's 39%, by the Linley Group's count. Altera, bought by Intel in 2015, became independent again in 2025 when Silver Lake took a majority stake; Microchip and Lattice fill out the rest. It's a small, high-margin market, about $11–12 billion a year, that rarely makes headlines.

There is no "chip champion." There are several separate races, and three companies that each win different ones.

So next time someone says a company "leads the chip market," ask two things.

QUESTION 1

Which segment do they actually lead?

QUESTION 2

Are they counting units or revenue, and which slice?
