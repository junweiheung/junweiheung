What Happens When You Press Send: Inside an AI Answer

Every reply from a chatbot is a short trip through a data centre: your words are cut into tokens, queued with thousands of other people’s, read in one burst and then answered one token at a time. Follow a single message along that path, see why “mixture-of-experts” models change the arithmetic, and why two chip start-ups, Groq and Taalas, bet on opposite ways to make it faster.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics
November 24, 2026 · 18 min read

This is a simplified picture of how large providers typically serve a language model; each company does it differently and few publish the details. The worked numbers are my own arithmetic from published specifications and model cards, and they are upper bounds at perfect efficiency: real systems are slower. Company claims are labelled as claims.

Key findings

  • An answer comes in two phases. Prefill3 reads your whole prompt at once and is limited by arithmetic; decode writes the answer one token at a time and is limited by how fast the chip can read memory.
  • For every token it writes, a chip must read essentially the whole model again. For a 70-billion-weight model stored at one byte per weight, that is 70 GB per token.
  • Mixture-of-experts models only use a slice of themselves per token. DeepSeek-V3 holds 671 billion8 weights but uses 37 billion per token, so it needs the memory of a giant and the arithmetic of a mid-sized model.
  • That saving shrinks when many users share a chip: their tokens call on different experts, and with 64 users in a batch, if tokens chose experts at random, about 87% of DeepSeek-V3’s experts would be read on every step.
  • Groq keeps models in fast on-chip memory spread over hundreds of chips; Nvidia licensed its technology in a deal reported at about $20 billion10 and now pairs Groq-designed chips with its GPUs. Taalas builds one model directly into the silicon; AMD bought it in November 2026.

Part 1One request, step by step

Training a model is a one-off project. Running it, which the industry calls inference, happens every time anyone asks anything, and over a popular model’s life it is usually the bigger bill. It is also where most of the engineering effort now goes, because the speed of every answer and the cost of every token is decided here.

Step 1 · Your device

The message

You type a question and press send. What leaves your phone is not just that line: the app packages the whole conversation so far, every earlier question and answer, because the model itself remembers nothing between requests. Each new message is, to the model, a brand-new document that happens to contain the history.

That is why long chats get slower and more expensive: every turn re-sends everything before it.

Step 2 · CPU servers

The front door: the API

The request arrives at the provider’s API, a set of ordinary servers. They check who you are and whether you have hit a usage limit, run safety filters, and add things you never see: a system prompt, the provider’s standing instructions to the model, and descriptions of any tools it may use.

None of this needs an AI chip. It is web-server work, done by the kind of processors AMD sells as EPYC and Intel as Xeon.

Step 3 · CPU servers

Tokenise: words become numbers

Models do not read letters or words. They read tokens: chunks of text from a fixed vocabulary, learned so that common words are one token and rare words are a few. Llama 3’s vocabulary has 128,2561 of them. Each token is swapped for its number in that list.

As a rule of thumb from OpenAI, a token is about four characters, or three-quarters of an English word. Other languages and code split differently. Tokens are also the unit everything is priced and measured in.

Step 4 · CPU servers

Route: pick a server

A load balancer sends the request to a data centre, and then to one group of servers that has the right model loaded and room to spare. Big models do not fit on one chip, so a “server” here is usually a box of eight accelerators working as one, or several such boxes.

Routing is smarter than it sounds. If you are continuing a conversation, sending you back to a server that has already processed the earlier part saves redoing that work. Providers pass the saving on: Anthropic, for example, charges about a tenth of the normal input price for text read from this prompt cache.

Step 5 · Host CPU

Schedule: join the batch

An accelerator is fastest when it serves many people at once, because reading the model from memory is the slow part and one read can serve a whole batch of users. So the server queues your request and slots it in with others.

Modern servers use continuous batching4: instead of waiting for a whole batch to finish, they add new requests and remove finished ones on every step. The technique comes from research systems such as Orca (2022) and vLLM (2023), and it is now standard.

The trade-off: a bigger batch means more answers per second for the provider, but each user’s words arrive a little slower, because every step now carries more users’ work. In engineers’ terms, throughput goes up and inter-token latency goes up with it.

Step 6 · Accelerator

Prefill: read the whole prompt at once

Now the AI chip starts. In prefill it pushes every token of your prompt through the model in one go. Each layer works out, for every token, a pair of summaries called keys and values, and stores them in the KV cache so they never have to be worked out again.

Because all the tokens go through together, the chip reads its weights once and does a great deal of arithmetic with them. Prefill is limited by compute. A rule of thumb is about two operations per weight per token5, so a 2,000-token prompt through a 70-billion-weight model is roughly 280 trillion operations: about 0.02 seconds on an eight-GPU Nvidia H200 server at perfect efficiency, longer in practice.

This is the pause before the first word appears, which engineers call time to first token (TTFT). Because prefill work grows with the prompt, pasting in a long document makes this wait longer.

Step 7 · Accelerator

Decode: one token at a time

Then the answer is written, and it can only be written one token at a time, because each token depends on the one before. For every single token the chip must read all of the model’s weights again, plus the KV cache of everything said so far, to do comparatively little arithmetic.

So decode is limited by memory bandwidth, how fast data comes out of memory, not by arithmetic. A 70-billion-weight model at one byte per weight means about 70 GB read per token; an eight-GPU H200 server reads 38.4 TB a second, which caps one user at roughly 550 tokens a second before any real-world losses.

Turned round, that is one token every 1.8 milliseconds. The gap between one token and the next is called inter-token latency (ITL), and it sets how fast the answer seems to type.

The KV cache grows as the conversation does. For Llama 3.1 70B it is about 0.33 MB per token, so a 100,000-token conversation carries around 33 GB of it, which must be read on every step too. That is why long chats can type more slowly.

Step 8 · Accelerator

Sample: choose the next word

Each pass through the model does not produce a word. It produces a score for every token in the vocabulary, all 128,256 of them for Llama 3, which become probabilities. The server then picks one.

A setting called temperature controls how adventurous the pick is: low temperature almost always takes the top choice, high temperature gives less likely tokens a real chance. That is why the same question can get different answers.

Step 9 · CPU servers

Detokenise and stream

The chosen token’s number is turned back into text and sent to you immediately, while decode carries on with the next one. That is why answers appear word by word: you are watching the decode loop in real time.

The loop stops when the model picks a special end-of-answer token, or hits a length limit.

Step 10 · Your device

The response

The plumbing (API, tokenising, routing, streaming) takes milliseconds. Almost all of the time, and almost all of the cost, sits in the two phases on the accelerator: a short burst of arithmetic, then a long relay of memory reads.

Which raises the question the rest of this page is about: if decode spends most of its time reading the model, what happens when you change the model, or change where it is stored?

Prefill is a sprint of arithmetic. Decode is a relay of memory reads, one lap per word.

Those two phases give the two numbers you actually feel when you use a chatbot. Time to first token is the wait before anything appears; inter-token latency is how fast it types once it starts. They are set by different phases, so they can go wrong separately: a reply can start instantly and then crawl, or sit silent for seconds and then pour out.

The two also get in each other’s way. If one chip does both phases, a newcomer’s long prefill can take over a step and stall every stream already decoding, a spike in everyone else’s ITL. Servers soften this by chopping prefill into chunks that ride alongside decode steps, or by putting prefill and decode on separate machines altogether, an idea that comes back in Part 4.

Part 2Dense and mixture-of-experts

A dense model uses every one of its weights for every token. Llama 3.1 70B is dense: 70 billion weights, all 70 billion used each time.

A mixture-of-experts (MoE) model splits most of its layers into many smaller blocks, the “experts”, and adds a small router that sends each token to only a few of them. Mixtral 8x7B has eight experts per layer and uses two; DeepSeek-V3 has 256 and uses eight, plus one shared expert every token visits. Most of the model sits idle for any given token.

The point is that two numbers now describe a model instead of one. Total weights decide how much memory you need to hold it. Active weights decide how much arithmetic each token costs, and, for a single user, how much must be read per token. DeepSeek-V3 has to be stored like a 671-billion-weight model but computes like a 37-billion one.

There is a catch, and it is about batching. One user’s token reads only its few experts. But a server batching 64 users is handling 64 tokens per step, each choosing its own experts, so between them they touch most of the model anyway. The memory-read saving that makes MoE fast for one person largely disappears when the chip is busy, which is exactly when providers make their money.

So MoE is not a free lunch. It cuts arithmetic per token sharply, which is why frontier labs have embraced it, but it makes memory capacity matter even more: you must hold every expert somewhere, and with many users you end up reading most of them. That is the problem the chip designers in Part 3 are attacking from opposite directions.

Part 3Three ways to build the chip

If decode is a memory problem, there are three places to put the model’s weights, and each has become a business.

Start with the usual one, a GPU. Its weights sit in high-bandwidth memory beside the processor, so the fastest one user can ever be served is simply the chip’s memory speed divided by the size of the model. For Llama 3.1 8B at 16 bits (about 16 GB) on an Nvidia H200 (4.86 TB/s), that is about 300 tokens a second; Nvidia measures about 230. AMD’s answer has been to sell more bandwidth.

More bandwidth raises the ceiling, but only so far: every token still means reading the whole model. The other two designs try to stop reading it from far away.

Groq: keep the model on the chip

Groq’s chip, the LPU, has no external high-bandwidth memory at all. It keeps the model in SRAM on the chip, the same kind of memory a processor uses for its fastest caches, which Groq says runs at more than 80 TB/s, many times what HBM delivers. The first-generation chip, made on a 14-nanometre process, holds just 230 MB9. So a model is spread across a great many chips that pass data along in a carefully timed pipeline: in 2023, Groq’s fast Llama 2 70B demonstration reportedly used 576 chips in nine racks.

It worked. Groq became known for answers that seemed to appear all at once, and in December 2025 Nvidia struck a non-exclusive deal to license its technology and hire its founder, reported at about $20 billion. By March 2026 Nvidia had turned it into a product: the Groq 3 LPX rack, 256 LP30 chips with 500 MB of SRAM each, 128 GB in all. In Nvidia’s reference design, GPUs do the prefill and the attention part of decode, while the LPUs handle the memory-hungry feed-forward layers, the very layers that MoE splits into experts.

The catch is capacity. 128 GB is a whole rack. The Register worked out that a trillion-weight model needs four to eight LPX racks, 1,024 to 2,048 chips, depending on whether weights are stored at 4 or 8 bits.

Taalas: make the model the chip

Taalas went further: why store the weights at all? Its HC1 chip, announced in February 2026, builds all of Llama 3.1 8B into the circuitry of one TSMC 6nm chip with 53 billion transistors, so the weights sit right where the arithmetic happens. Taalas claims about 17,000 tokens11 a second for a single user; independent testers measured 15,000 to 16,000, about 70 times an H200.

The cost is flexibility. The chip runs one model, some quality is lost squeezing its weights small, and a new model needs a new chip, which Taalas says takes about two months. AMD agreed to buy the company in August 2026 and completed the deal in November. That story is told in the AMD explainer.

Part 4Where it all meets

Put Parts 2 and 3 together and the industry’s direction makes sense. Mixture-of-experts suits GPUs well: they have room for every expert and only need to do the arithmetic for the active few. It is awkward for Groq-style chips, because every expert still needs scarce SRAM, so the chip count grows with the total model, not the active slice. And it is hardest for Taalas-style chips, which would have to etch every expert into silicon.

So nobody is betting on one design. The trend is to split the work: give prefill, which wants arithmetic, to GPUs, and give decode, which wants memory speed, to something built for it. Nvidia’s GPU-plus-LPX racks do exactly that. A bonus is that a newcomer’s long prompt no longer stalls everyone else’s typing: time to first token and inter-token latency can each be tuned on their own hardware. Reported ideas around AMD’s Taalas purchase, not AMD announcements, include GPUs doing prefill and Taalas chips doing decode.

For a reader, the useful habit is to ask two questions of any claim about a faster AI chip. Fast for one user, or for many at once? And fast on which model, at what size, and could it run a different one tomorrow?
