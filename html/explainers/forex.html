The $9.6 Trillion Market

Every day about $9.6 trillion of currencies changes hands, more than any other market on earth. Most of it is banks and funds you never see; a sliver is people at home trading on their phones with borrowed money. Here is who trades forex, how to read a currency pair, why leverage pulls traders in, and what happens when a currency breaks.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics
November 24, 2026 · 11 min read

Key findings

  • Foreign exchange is the world’s largest market: about $9.6 trillion a day in April 2025, up 28% in three years, by the Bank for International Settlements’ count. It has no central exchange. Big dealer banks quote prices to each other and to everyone else.
  • The US dollar is on one side of 89% of all trades. London handles 38% of the world’s turnover, and Singapore 11.8%, a bigger share than ever.
  • Every trade is a pair: buying one currency means selling another. Prices move in pips, usually a hundredth of a cent, which is why retail traders use leverage. At 20:1, the cap for Singapore retail traders, a 5% move against you wipes out your whole deposit.
  • When a fixed rate breaks, the move can be enormous. In January 2015 the Swiss franc jumped by up to about 30% in minutes, leaving traders owing more than they had deposited and pushing a major broker to an emergency rescue.

Part 1Who trades

There is no building where currencies are traded, no equivalent of the New York Stock Exchange. Forex is an over-the-counter market: a network of dealers who quote prices to each other and to their clients, by electronic platform and, still sometimes, by phone. Trading follows the sun, from Sydney and Tokyo to Singapore, London and New York, five and a half days a week.

The market is layered. At its core sit a few dozen dealer banks, which make prices and hold the risk. Around them are other financial institutions: smaller banks, hedge funds, pension funds and investment managers. Further out are companies paying for imports or bringing home foreign profits, and central banks, which manage reserves and sometimes intervene to steady their currency. At the edge are retail brokers, which bundle up the small trades of individual investors.

The numbers are hard to picture. The BIS surveys central banks every three years, and in April 2025 they counted about $9.6 trillion a day. The largest slice, around $4 trillion, was not spot trading but FX swaps: deals that swap currencies today and swap them back later, which banks and companies use to borrow one currency against another. Plain spot trading, the kind a tourist or a retail trader does, grew fastest, up 42% in three years.

Geography matters too. London handles 38% of global turnover, a lead it has held for decades because its time zone overlaps both Asia and New York. Singapore took 11.8% in 2025, up from 9.5% in 2022, cementing its place as Asia’s largest currency hub.

The dollar’s 89% share is not just about America’s size. It is the market’s vehicle currency: someone swapping Thai baht for Mexican pesos usually does it in two legs, baht to dollars and dollars to pesos, because both legs are deep and cheap, while a direct baht–peso market barely exists. That habit is part of what keeps the dollar at the centre of the system, the subject of the seigniorage story.

Part 2Reading a pair

A currency is always priced in another currency, so forex trades in pairs. In EUR/USD 1.1600, the euro is the base currency and the dollar the quote currency: one euro costs 1.16 dollars. Buying EUR/USD means buying euros and paying in dollars; selling it means the reverse. Every trade is therefore long one currency and short another.

Dealers show two prices, a lower bid they will pay and a higher ask they will sell at. The gap is the spread, and for most retail traders it is the main cost: many brokers charge no separate commission, because they are paid in the spread instead. Price moves are counted in pips, the fourth decimal place for most pairs (0.0001) and the second for yen pairs (0.01), because a yen is worth so little. Trades come in lots: a standard lot is 100,000 units of the base currency, a mini lot 10,000 and a micro lot 1,000, and many brokers allow sizes in between.

Majors, crosses and exoticsTraders sort pairs into three groups. Majors pair the US dollar with another big, heavily traded currency: the euro, yen, pound, Swiss franc, Canadian, Australian or New Zealand dollar. Crosses leave the dollar out entirely, such as EUR/JPY or GBP/JPY; they are sometimes loosely called “minors”. Exotics pair the dollar or euro with a smaller or emerging-market currency, such as USD/THB or USD/SGD, and usually carry wider spreads.

Part 3The pull, and the catch

Brokers sell forex on a familiar list of advantages, and most of them are real. The market is deep: in the major pairs you can trade large amounts without moving the price. It runs around the clock on weekdays. Costs are low, mostly a spread of a pip or so on the majors. Going short is as easy as going long, since selling one currency is simply buying another. Sizes are flexible, down to micro lots. And brokers offer leverage: you put down a deposit, called margin, and control a position many times larger.

Leverage is the reason the rest matters. Major currencies rarely move more than about half a percent on an ordinary day, so without borrowing, a forex trade earns or loses very little. Leverage multiplies that, in both directions, and the arithmetic is unforgiving.

At 20:1, a 5% move against you erases the deposit; at 500:1, a move of 0.2%, an ordinary few hours, does it. That is why regulators stepped in. When the European Securities and Markets Authority capped retail leverage in 2018, it reported that 74% to 89% of retail accounts trading these products typically lost money. Brokers in the EU must now print their own figure on their websites, and it is usually in that range.

Leverage turns half a percent into a living. It also turns half a percent into a wipe-out.

Part 4When a currency breaks

Most of the time currencies drift. The violent moves come when a government has fixed its rate and the market decides it can’t hold. Then the trade becomes one-sided: if the peg holds, a short seller loses a little; if it breaks, the seller wins big. Three episodes show the pattern, and its mirror image.

1992 · Sterling

Breaking the Bank of England

Britain had tied the pound to the German mark in Europe’s Exchange Rate Mechanism, at a rate many traders thought too high. George Soros’s Quantum Fund bet billions against it. On 16 September, “Black Wednesday”, the Bank of England raised rates and bought pounds, then gave up; Britain left the mechanism that evening. Soros’s fund was reported to have made about $1 billion.

1997 · Thai baht

The peg that started a crisis

Thailand held the baht near 25 to the dollar while its foreign borrowing piled up. Hedge funds, Soros’s among them, sold the baht short; the central bank spent most of its usable reserves defending it, then floated it on 2 July. The baht soon lost about half its value and the panic spread across Asia. The full story is in the Asian crisis explainer.

2015 · Swiss franc

The floor that vanished

Here the leveraged traders were on the wrong side. Since 2011 the Swiss National Bank had capped the franc at 1.20 per euro, and many retail traders bet the cap would hold. On 15 January the bank dropped it without warning. The franc jumped by up to about 30% within minutes, prices skipped past stop-loss orders, and clients of the broker FXCM ended up owing it about $225 million. FXCM needed a $300 million rescue loan from Leucadia to stay in business.

The franc is the lesson the brochures skip. A stop-loss order only works if there is a price to fill it at; in a gap, there isn’t. With 50:1 leverage, a 30% move is not a loss of the deposit but fifteen times the deposit. Since then many regulators, including ESMA, have required brokers to offer negative-balance protection, so retail clients can’t lose more than they put in.

Part 5Reading the crowd

Because forex has no central exchange, nobody sees the whole order book. Traders look for clues in positioning: who is long and who is short. One popular retail source is Myfxbook’s Community Outlook, which shows, for each pair, what share of the retail accounts linked to the site are long or short, along with their average entry prices.

The contrarian reading has a logic: retail traders tend to buy into falling prices, hoping for a turn, and to take profits early, so a crowd that is heavily long can be a sign of a market still sliding. But it is a habit, not a law. The sample is only the accounts that chose to link to one website, not the market, and retail trading is a small part of the total. For a wider view, traders also watch the US Commodity Futures Trading Commission’s weekly Commitments of Traders report, which shows how speculators and hedgers are positioned in currency futures.

Which brings the story back to the rings in Part 1. The retail crowd sits at the edge of a market built around banks, funds and central banks, trading the same prices with far less information and far more leverage. Knowing where you sit is the first thing to understand about forex.
