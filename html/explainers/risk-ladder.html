The Saver's Ladder

A fixed deposit, a Singapore Savings Bond, a corporate bond, an ETF and a single stock are all places to park the same dollar. Each step up offers more expected return and asks you to accept more ways to lose. Here is what you actually own on each rung, who promises what, and what 125 years of history say about the climb.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics
November 25, 2026 · 12 min read

This explains how common savings and investment products work, using Singapore examples. Rates on deposits, Singapore Savings Bonds and T-bills change every month; long-run returns are historical averages, not forecasts. Products are named as examples only. Nothing here is investment advice or a recommendation to buy anything; for decisions about your own money, consider a licensed adviser.

Key findings

  • Every savings product trades off three things: expected return, risk (how far and how often its value can fall) and liquidity (how fast you can get cash out without a penalty). No product wins on all three.
  • The bottom rungs are guaranteed in dollars: Singapore bank deposits are insured up to S$100,0001 per depositor per bank, and Singapore Savings Bonds and T-bills are backed by the government. Their quiet risk is inflation, which can make a guaranteed dollar buy less.
  • Higher rungs pay more because they can fail. About 34,0005 mostly retail investors held some S$900 million of Hyflux perpetual securities and preference shares when the water company collapsed in 2018.
  • Over 1900–2024, world stocks returned about 5.2%7 a year above inflation, against 1.7% for bonds and 0.5% for cash-like bills. But a study of US stocks found 58% did worse than Treasury bills over their lifetimes, and just 4% produced all the market's net gains, which is the case for owning many companies rather than a few.

Part 1Three things you trade off

Put S$10,000 somewhere and you are making a trade, whether you think of it that way or not. You are choosing how much you hope to earn, how much the value can drop along the way, and how quickly you can turn it back into cash. Those three, return, risk and liquidity, pull against each other. A product that is safe and instantly available pays little, because the bank or government taking your money can count on keeping it only briefly and promising you everything back. A product that might pay a lot does so because something can go wrong.

There is a fourth thing that sits under all three: inflation. A dollar that is guaranteed to stay a dollar can still lose value, because prices rise. In Singapore, the central bank's core inflation measure peaked at about 5.5%4 in early 2023. Money earning 2% that year was safe in dollars and still lost about 3% of its buying power. So “safe” always means safe from something.

Picture the choices as a ladder. Each rung up, you are further from a guarantee and closer to owning a business outright. Tap a rung to see what you actually hold.

Two dials, not oneThe ladder ranks lending versus owning: for the same company, its bond is safer than its shares, because lenders are paid first. A second dial is how many companies you hold. A single company's bond can lose most of its value if that one company fails, while a broad stock fund holding thousands of companies can fall 40% in a bad year but would need all of them to fail together to go to zero. So one company's bond can carry more risk of permanent loss than a broad stock ETF, and a narrow single-theme ETF can swing like one stock. The order here reflects expected return and typical price swings, not every kind of risk.

Part 2The guaranteed rungs

The bottom of the ladder is where your claim is fixed in dollars and someone very solid stands behind it.

A savings account is a loan to your bank that you can call back at any moment. The bank lends most of it on, which is why it pays you anything at all. A fixed deposit is the same loan with a lock: you promise to leave the money for, say, six or twelve months, and in exchange the bank usually pays a higher rate. Break the lock early and you typically lose some or all of the interest, though not your principal. Both are covered by Singapore's deposit insurance scheme, run by the SDIC, up to S$100,000 per depositor at each member bank, a limit raised from S$75,000 in April 2024. Above that, you are relying on the bank itself.

One step over sit loans to the Singapore government. Singapore Savings Bonds (SSBs) are made for individuals: you can put in as little as S$5002, they last up to ten years with interest that steps up the longer you hold, and you can get your money back in any month with no penalty. Treasury bills are six-month or one-year loans sold at auction; you buy at a discount and get the full face value at the end. Both are backed by a government with a top credit rating, so the risk of not being repaid is about as low as it gets. Lending to Governments explains how the three government options differ, and why Singapore borrows money it doesn't need.

Many Singaporeans also hold a large guaranteed balance without choosing it: their CPF. The government guarantees a floor of 2.5%3 on the Ordinary Account and 4% on the Special and Retirement accounts. It is not liquid in the usual sense, since it is locked mainly for housing and retirement, but it is part of the same bottom of the ladder.

The guarantee is in dollars, not in buying powerEverything on these rungs promises you a fixed number of dollars. That makes them right for money you will need on a date, and for an emergency fund. It also means their real return, after inflation, has historically been thin: cash-like bills returned about 0.5% a year above inflation across 125 years of world markets. Safety from loss and growth over time are different jobs.

Part 3Lending to companies

A corporate bond is the same idea as a government bond, a loan with fixed interest and a date to be repaid, but the borrower is a company. Companies can and do fail, so they must pay more than the government to borrow. That extra yield is not a gift; it is the price of credit risk. The weaker the borrower, the higher the yield, and the larger the chance it is never paid.

Singapore learned this in public with Hyflux, once a celebrated water-treatment company. In 2016 it sold S$500 million of 6% perpetual securities to the public, in amounts small enough for retail savers. Perpetuals look like bonds but are weaker: they have no fixed date for repayment, the issuer can often defer interest, and in a collapse they are paid after ordinary lenders. When Hyflux went into court-supervised restructuring in 2018, about 34,000 investors, mostly individuals, held some S$900 million of its perpetuals and preference shares. Years of negotiation followed, and those holders recovered only a small fraction of what they had put in.

The lesson isn't that company bonds are bad; large, stable companies borrow this way all the time. It is that a 6% coupon when banks pay 1–2% is telling you something. A yield above the safe rate is always payment for a risk, and the question is whether you can see which one.

Hyflux also shows the second dial at work. Its holders had lent to one company, so one failure was enough. A bond fund or bond ETF spreads the same money across hundreds of borrowers, so a single default costs a sliver rather than most of the stake, the same protection a broad stock ETF gives against any one company going under. Bond funds still fall when interest rates rise, as they did in 2022, but a fund that keeps buying new bonds earns the higher yields back over time; a default is simply gone.

Part 4Owning companies

At the top of the ladder you stop lending and start owning. A share is a slice of a company: you get no promised interest and no repayment date, only a claim on whatever profits are left after everyone else is paid, through dividends and a rising share price. Because owners stand last in line, they demand the highest expected return, and over long periods they have got it.

But that long-run return belongs to the market as a whole, and the average hides an uncomfortable fact about the individual pieces. The finance professor Hendrik Bessembinder studied6 every US common stock from 1926 to 2016. Most of them did not even beat one-month Treasury bills over their listed lifetimes. All of the stock market's net gain above Treasury bills came from about one in twenty-five companies.

Pick a handful of stocks and the odds are that you miss most of that rare 4%. That is the argument for an index fund or an exchange-traded fund (ETF): a single product, bought on the stock exchange like a share, that holds hundreds or thousands of companies at once. An STI ETF holds the 30 companies in Singapore's Straits Times Index; a global ETF can hold thousands across dozens of countries. You give up the chance of picking the next giant and in return you are sure to own it, along with all the duds.

Not all ETFs are equally diversified. A fund that holds only one sector, one country or one theme can swing as wildly as a single stock. And they all charge a yearly fee, the expense ratio, taken quietly from the fund's value. Part 5What time does

Over a single year, the top of the ladder can be painful. World stocks fell by roughly 40% in 20088. Even bonds, usually the calm rung, had a terrible 2022, when rising interest rates knocked about 16% off a broad global bond index. Over a long stretch, though, the ranking has been remarkably stable. The researchers Elroy Dimson, Paul Marsh and Mike Staunton track returns for markets around the world back to 1900. Their figures, after inflation, are what the calculator uses.

Two things stand out. The gaps between rungs are small in any one year and enormous over decades, because returns compound on returns. And fees compound too: at 1.5% a year, a fund hands a large share of the stock market's real return to its manager. Time is what lets the top of the ladder work, and it is exactly what money for next month's rent doesn't have.

Part 6Matching rung to purpose

So the question is rarely “which rung is best?” but “what is this money for, and when?” The usual way people and planners think about it lines each pot of money up with the rung that suits its job.

The ladder is the same idea that runs through the rest of this series. A government that borrows cheaply, a bank that lends out deposits, a company that issues shares: each sits on one end of a trade where someone else is on your rung. Knowing who is on the other side, and what they promised, is most of what it takes to read any financial product. For how the government end works, see Lending to Governments and One Seesaw, Two Fears; for products further out on the risk curve, see How Derivatives Work.
