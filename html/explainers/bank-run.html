The Rumour That Emptied a Bank

In December 1973 a schoolgirl's joke on a Japanese train sent thousands of people to queue outside a healthy bank. A bank run happens when depositors rush to take their money out because they fear the bank can't repay them. How banks lend out most of what you deposit, why a cash shortage can turn into a collapse, and the tools governments use to keep the queues away.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics

Key findings

  • A bank run happens when many depositors rush to withdraw at once because they fear the bank can't repay them. It can start with nothing more than a rumour: in 1973 a joke between three schoolgirls set off a run on Toyokawa Shinkin Bank in Japan.
  • Banks keep only a fraction of deposits as cash and lend or invest the rest. That's how they pay interest and fund loans, but it means no bank can pay every depositor at once.
  • A run turns a liquidity problem, not enough cash today, into a solvency problem: to raise cash fast, the bank sells assets cheaply, and the losses can wipe out its capital. A bank that was sound can be broken by the run itself.
  • Runs are self-fulfilling: if you expect others to withdraw, withdrawing first is the sensible thing to do. The economists who modelled this, Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig, shared the 2022 Nobel prize.
  • Phones made runs faster: Silicon Valley Bank lost about $42 billion of deposits in a single day in March 2023. Governments respond with deposit insurance (S$100,000 per depositor per bank in Singapore), emergency lending, liquidity and capital rules, and, when all else fails, rescues.

Part 1A joke on a train

On the morning of 8 December 1973, three high-school girls were chatting on a local train in Aichi Prefecture, central Japan. One of them had a job lined up after graduation at Toyokawa Shinkin Bank, a small local savings bank. Her friends teased her that the bank was "dangerous". They meant a bank clerk might get held up by robbers. She took it to heart, and that evening asked a relative whether a shinkin bank was really dangerous.

Within five days the question had become a statement, and the statement had become a queue. Japanese researchers later traced the rumour person by person, which is why the case is still taught as a study in how panic spreads1. Nothing was wrong with the bank.

The timing helped. A month earlier, the oil shock had set off a panic in which Japanese shoppers stripped shelves of toilet paper. People were primed to believe that things might run out. But the deeper reason the rumour worked is that it was, in one sense, true: no bank could have paid all its depositors at once. Not Toyokawa Shinkin, and not yours.

Part 2Where your deposit goes

When you put $100 in a bank, the bank doesn't keep $100 in a vault. It keeps a small part as cash, or as money in its own account at the central bank, and uses the rest to make loans and buy bonds. This is fractional-reserve banking: banks keep only a fraction of customer deposits as reserves, and hold the rest in loans and investments.

It's the whole point of a bank. Your deposit funds someone's mortgage or a company's expansion, and the interest on those loans pays the interest on your account. It works because, on any normal day, only a few depositors want their money back, and new deposits come in to replace them. Many countries once set a minimum fraction that banks had to keep as reserves. The US cut its requirement to zero in March 20202, relying instead on the liquidity rules described in Part 6.

The catch is a mismatch of timing. Deposits can be withdrawn today; a mortgage is repaid over 30 years. A bank is solvent if what it owns is worth more than what it owes. It is liquid if it can pay what's due right now. A bank can be the first without being the second.

Part 3From cash shortage to collapse

Here is how a run breaks a bank. When withdrawals outrun its cash, the bank has to raise money fast. It sells bonds first, since they're easy to sell. If rates have risen since it bought them, they're worth less than it paid, and selling turns a paper loss into a real one. Then it has to sell loans, which buyers will only take at a steep discount if they're bought in a hurry. Every loss comes out of the bank's capital, the owners' money that absorbs losses before depositors do. When capital runs out, the bank is insolvent.

Try the same run with the central bank lending instead. The bank pledges its loans rather than dumping them, so it raises the cash without taking a loss, and the run can pass. That is the logic of the lender of last resort, the oldest tool against runs, and it's why the Bank of Japan's cash display in Toyokawa worked: it showed depositors that money would not run out.

A bank can survive almost anything except everyone wanting their money back on the same day.

Part 4Why panic spreads

The uncomfortable truth about a run is that joining it can be the sensible thing to do. Banks pay depositors in the order they turn up. If everyone else stays calm, you lose nothing by waiting. If everyone else runs, the depositors at the front of the queue get paid in full and the ones at the back may wait months, or lose money. So whether you should withdraw depends less on the bank's health than on what you think other people will do.

In 1983 the economists Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig set this out in a model that showed runs could hit a perfectly healthy bank, as a self-fulfilling prophecy, and that deposit insurance could prevent them. Ben Bernanke showed how bank failures deepened the Great Depression. The three shared the 2022 Nobel prize in economics3.

Part 5Runs that made history

Runs have taken many forms: crowds on the pavement, a frenzy of online transfers, or wealthy clients quietly moving money abroad. Four stand out.

1930–1933 · United States

The Great Depression

Waves of panic swept American banks after late 1930, as depositors tried to turn deposits into cash. About 9,0004 banks suspended operations, and the Federal Reserve did little to supply the cash they needed.

What changed: the Banking Act of 1933 created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. Runs on American banks became rare for decades.
September 2007 · Britain

Northern Rock

The mortgage lender funded itself by borrowing in markets that froze when the US housing crisis began. When news broke that it had asked the Bank of England for emergency support, savers queued outside its branches5: the first run on a British bank in well over a century.

What happened: the news of the rescue set off the run it was meant to prevent. The government guaranteed deposits and nationalised the bank in early 2008.
March 2023 · United States

Silicon Valley Bank

SVB had put its deposits into long-term bonds that lost value as rates rose. Most of its depositors were tech start-ups and their investors, with balances far above the insured limit, who talked to each other and moved money by phone.

What happened: depositors withdrew about $42 billion6 on 9 March and asked for about $100 billion more the next morning. Regulators closed the bank on 10 March. The Fed's own review found its supervisors had not fully appreciated the risks.
March 2023 · Switzerland

Credit Suisse

Years of losses and scandals had worn down trust in the 167-year-old bank. After SVB failed, wealthy clients moved money out quickly. Clients pulled a net CHF 61.2 billion7 in the first three months of 2023, most of it in the second half of March.

What happened: on 19 March the Swiss authorities arranged a takeover by its rival UBS, backed by huge loans from the Swiss National Bank.

SVB showed how much faster runs had become. At Toyokawa the rumour took five days to reach the bank's counters, and depositors had to queue in person. In 2023, a run that would once have taken weeks happened in hours, organised in group chats and carried out on phones.

Part 6How governments stop runs

No government can make every bank able to pay every depositor at once. What it can do is give depositors fewer reasons to run, and give banks ways to get cash without selling everything. There are five main tools.

Part 7What it means for your savings

For most savers in Singapore, the practical lesson is short. Singapore-dollar deposits at a full bank or finance company are insured up to S$100,000 per depositor per bank12, by the Singapore Deposit Insurance Corporation. All your accounts at one bank count together towards that limit, but money at a different bank gets its own S$100,000. Foreign-currency deposits, structured deposits and investments such as shares and unit trusts aren't covered.

So if you hold more than the limit, spreading it across banks keeps all of it insured. And the next time you hear that a bank is in trouble, it's worth remembering Toyokawa: the bank was fine, the rumour wasn't true, and the people who rushed to withdraw took the trouble of queueing for money that had never been at risk.
