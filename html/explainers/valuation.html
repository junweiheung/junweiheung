What Is a Company Worth?

A share price on its own says almost nothing. Is $500 expensive? Is a P/E of 40 a bubble? Valuation ratios turn a price into a comparison: what you pay for each dollar of sales, profit or cash. Here is what each common ratio measures, when to use which, and where the numbers come from.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics
November 25, 2026 · 14 min read

This explains how common valuation ratios work. The ranges quoted are rough conventions that vary by industry and era, not buy or sell signals. Companies are named as examples only. Nothing here is investment advice or a recommendation to buy or sell anything.

Key findings

  • Market cap (share price × shares) is what the stock market says the owners’ stake is worth. Enterprise value adds debt and subtracts cash: roughly what it would cost to buy the whole company and settle what it owes. Two companies with the same market cap can have very different enterprise values.
  • The P/E ratio is what you pay for each dollar of last year’s profit. Forward P/E uses analysts’ forecasts instead, and the PEG ratio divides P/E by expected growth, so a high P/E can still be reasonable for a fast grower. The S&P 500 traded at about 22 times forecast earnings at the end of 2025, against a 10-year average near 18.5.
  • For companies without profits, investors use EV/Revenue; to compare companies with different debts and tax bills, EV/EBITDA. EBITDA is a rough stand-in for operating cash, not cash itself: Charlie Munger suggested reading it as “bullshit earnings”.
  • “Normal” multiples differ hugely by industry. At the start of 2026, US semiconductor companies traded at about 16 times sales on Aswath Damodaran’s figures, several times the level for most other industries. Compare a company with its peers and its own history, not with the whole market.

Part 1Price times shares

A share price is meaningless on its own, because companies divide themselves into very different numbers of shares. A company with a $500 share price and 100 million shares is smaller than one with a $50 share price and 10 billion. The first useful number is market capitalisation: the share price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding.

Market capShare price × Shares outstanding$50 × 1 billion shares = $50 billion

Market cap is the stock market’s price for the owners’ stake, the equity. It is what decides a company’s weight in most stock indexes, as Who Gets the Biggest Slice? explains, and it is how companies are sorted by size. The usual US bands are rough: mega-cap above about $200 billion1, large-cap $10–200 billion, mid-cap $2–10 billion, small-cap about $250 million to $2 billion, and micro-cap below that. Size says something about risk, since small companies tend to swing more, but nothing about whether the price is fair.

Its blind spot is the rest of the balance sheet. Market cap ignores what the company owes and the cash it holds. A company worth $50 billion on the stock market that also owes $30 billion is a very different proposition from one worth $50 billion with no debt and a pile of cash.

Why companies split their shares

The clearest proof that the share price alone means little is a stock split. In a 10-for-1 split, every shareholder gets ten new shares for each old one, and the price falls to a tenth. The company, its profits and your slice of it are exactly the same; only the number of pieces changes. Nvidia2 split 10-for-1 in June 2024, Chipotle 50-for-1 the same month after its shares passed $3,000, and Walmart 3-for-1 that February.

So why bother? Companies usually give three reasons. The first is affordability: a $50 share is easier for a small investor to buy than a $500 one, and employees paid in stock get round numbers. The second is liquidity, the idea that a lower price brings in more buyers and sellers. The evidence here is mixed: going back to a well-known 1979 study by Thomas Copeland3, researchers have often found that the bid–ask spread, measured as a share of the price, gets wider after a split, because spreads don’t shrink in proportion to the price. The third is confidence. Companies tend to split after a long rise, so a split signals that management expects the higher price to hold; that is a signal about the business, not something the split itself creates.

The affordability case has weakened. Since around 2019 many brokers have let customers buy fractions of a share, so a $3,000 share can be bought $50 at a time. That is one reason some companies never split: Berkshire Hathaway’s Class A shares, which have never been split, trade for hundreds of thousands of dollars each. A split still matters in one place, the price-weighted Dow, where a lower share price means a smaller weight, as Who Gets the Biggest Slice? explains.

Part 2The takeover price

Imagine buying the whole company. You would pay the shareholders the market cap, but you would also take on its debts, which someone has to repay, and you would get its cash, which you could use to pay some of them off. That gives enterprise value (EV), sometimes called the takeover price.

Enterprise valueMarket cap + Total debt − Cash$50B + $20B debt − $5B cash = $65 billion

EV is the value of the whole business, whoever financed it, owners or lenders. That makes it the better starting point for comparing companies that borrow very differently, such as an airline or a utility funded mostly by debt against a software company sitting on cash. Move the sliders to see two companies with the same market cap pull apart.

On market cap alone the two look identical. Measured by EV, the indebted company is far more expensive per dollar of business, and it carries the extra risk that its lenders get paid first if things go wrong. That is why many analysts prefer EV-based ratios for debt-heavy industries such as manufacturing, telecoms, airlines and utilities.

Part 3Paying for profits

The most quoted ratio of all is the price-to-earnings ratio (P/E). It divides the share price by the profit per share, so it tells you how many dollars you are paying for each dollar of annual profit.

P/EShare price ÷ Earnings per shareor Market cap ÷ Net profit · $100 ÷ $5 = 20

Another way to read it: at a P/E of 20, if profits never grew, it would take 20 years of earnings to add up to the price. Investors accept high P/Es when they expect profits to grow fast, and demand low ones when they doubt the profits will last. The trailing P/E uses the past 12 months of actual profit. The forward P/E uses analysts’ estimates for the next 12 months, which matters for fast-growing companies: a stock that looks expensive on last year’s profit can look reasonable on next year’s.

To judge whether a high P/E is justified by growth, investors use the PEG ratio: the P/E divided by the expected annual growth in earnings, in percent. A P/E of 40 with profits growing 40% a year gives a PEG of 1, often read as fair value. The calculator puts the three together.

P/E has three well-known weaknesses. It is useless when a company makes a loss, since a negative P/E means nothing. It looks backwards, unless you use forecasts, and forecasts are often wrong. And it ignores debt, because it is built on market cap. A low P/E can be a bargain, or a sign that profits are about to fall; banks in a crisis and companies in decline often look “cheap”.

A market-wide readingThe ratio works for whole markets too. FactSet put the S&P 500 at about 22 times the next 12 months4’ forecast earnings at the end of 2025, above its 5-year average of about 20 and its 10-year average of about 18.5. By November 2026 it had fallen to about 17.7, but mostly because forecast earnings jumped, not because prices fell: P/E can drop while a market rises.

Part 4Before the profits

Many young companies, in technology and biotech especially, make no profit yet. Their P/E is meaningless, so investors fall back on sales. EV/Revenue (or its cousin, price-to-sales) tells you how much you are paying for each dollar of revenue, with debt included.

EV/RevenueEnterprise value ÷ Revenue$65B ÷ $20B revenue = 3.25×

Its weakness is the mirror image of its use: it ignores profitability. A company can grow revenue fast while losing money on every sale. EV/Revenue makes most sense alongside the company’s margins, and when comparing companies in the same business.

One step down the income statement is EBITDA: earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortisation. It starts from net profit and adds back the interest paid on debt, the tax bill, and the accounting charges for wearing out equipment (depreciation) and using up intangible assets such as patents (amortisation).

EBITDANet profit + Interest + Taxes + Depreciation + AmortisationA rough measure of operating cash, before financing and accounting choices

Stripping those out makes companies with different debts, tax countries and accounting easier to compare, which is why EV/EBITDA is a favourite of bankers pricing takeovers. But EBITDA is not cash. It ignores the money a company must spend on new equipment, the cash tied up in stock and unpaid bills, and the debt it has to repay. Companies also like to publish “adjusted” EBITDA that adds back supposedly one-off costs, some of which recur every year. Charlie Munger, Warren Buffett5’s partner, said that every time you see the word EBITDA you should substitute “bullshit earnings”. The safer check is free cash flow: the cash from operations left after capital spending.

Part 5Which ratio, when

No single number tells the whole story. Each ratio answers a different question, and the right one depends on what kind of company you are looking at.

The ranges people quote for each ratio are only rough guides, and industry matters more than any rule of thumb. At the start of 2026, Aswath Damodaran of New York University calculated that US semiconductor companies traded at about 15.76 times revenue and 35 times EBITDA on average, levels that would look absurd for a supermarket or a steelmaker. For banks, whose business is borrowing and lending, debt is the raw material rather than a burden, so EV-based ratios make little sense; investors use price-to-book (market cap ÷ the accounting value of shareholders’ equity) instead.

Four common mistakesComparing across industries: a software company and a utility will never have similar P/Es. Ignoring debt: a low P/E can hide a heavy loan book; check EV. Using P/E on a loss-maker: switch to EV/Revenue or price-to-book. Looking at one ratio only: combine it with growth, margins, cash flow and the quality of management.

Part 6Where the numbers come from

Every ratio is built from figures in a company’s own reports. Knowing which document holds which number makes it much easier to check a claim yourself.

A practical order of reading is the earnings release for the headline, the call for the explanation, and the full 10-Q or 10-K, or the SGX half-yearly report, for the detail and the footnotes, where the debt, the one-off items and the share count actually live. Those are the inputs; the ratios are just ways of dividing one by another. For the other school, which ignores the business and studies the price chart itself, see Reading the Squiggles. Shrinking the Pie shows one way the share count, and so earnings per share, can change without the business changing at all.

A few free places to get the numbers: SEC EDGAR9 for the filings themselves; Stock Analysis10 for a company's P/E, EV and EBITDA already worked out; Aswath Damodaran's datasets11 (NYU Stern) for typical multiples by industry, updated each January; and multpl12 for the S&P 500's P/E back to the 1870s. A ratio means most next to its industry and its own history.
