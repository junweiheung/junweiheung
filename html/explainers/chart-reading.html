Reading the Squiggles

Open any trading app and the price comes wrapped in tools: candlesticks, moving averages, an RSI line hovering between 30 and 70. This is technical analysis, the practice of reading price and volume history for clues about what comes next. Here is how the main charts and indicators are built, what traders look for in them, and what the evidence says.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics
November 25, 2026 · 16 min read

Key findings

  • Technical analysis studies past prices and volume to judge where a price may go; fundamental analysis studies the business. Many investors use both: fundamentals for what to buy, charts for when.
  • The same prices can be drawn very differently. Candlesticks and OHLC bars show every period’s open, high, low and close. Point and Figure, Kagi and Renko charts drop time altogether and only move when the price moves by a set amount, filtering out noise.
  • Traders read patterns in both: on candlesticks, shapes such as the hammer, engulfing candle and morning star; on Point & Figure, breakouts such as the double-top buy signal. Most are reversal signals that need context and confirmation.
  • A golden cross is the 50-day moving average rising above the 200-day; a death cross is the reverse. Momentum tools such as RSI (overbought above 70, oversold below 30) and MACD try to measure how fast a move is going and whether it is tiring.
  • The evidence is mixed. A famous 1992 study found simple moving-average rules beat the market on a century of Dow data, but later work found the edge largely vanished after trading costs and faded once it was published.

Part 1Two ways to judge a stock

There are two broad ways to decide whether a share is worth buying. Fundamental analysis looks at the business: its revenue, profits, debt and how its price compares, the ratios in What Is a Company Worth?. It asks what the company is worth. Technical analysis looks only at the market’s own record, the history of prices and trading volume, and asks what the crowd is doing and whether that is likely to continue.

The idea behind it is that prices move in trends that persist for a while, that crowds repeat their behaviour, and that some of that behaviour leaves patterns in the price. Technical tools are most popular with short-term traders, in currencies and crypto as well as stocks, but plenty of long-term investors glance at a chart before buying. Everything starts with how the prices are drawn.

Part 2Five ways to draw the same prices

A candlestick chart, the default in most apps, draws one candle per period, a day here. The thick body runs from the opening price to the closing price, coloured up or down; thin wicks show the high and low. A bar chart, usually called OHLC for open, high, low and close, shows the same four numbers as a vertical line from the low to the high, with a tick to the left at the open and a tick to the right at the close. Many traders find candles quicker to read because the coloured body makes the day’s direction obvious; OHLC bars are thinner, so more days fit on screen. Candlesticks are said1 to trace back to Japanese rice traders in the 18th century and became popular in the West in the 1990s.

The other three throw away the calendar. They only add something when the price has moved by a set amount, so quiet days leave no mark. Switch between them below: it is the same 160 days of prices each time.

Candlestick patterns: a cheatsheet

Candlestick traders read the shape of one to three candles as a sign of who won the session: a long lower wick means sellers pushed the price down and buyers pushed it back; a big body that swallows the previous one means one side took over. Most patterns are reversal signals and only mean something in context: a hammer after a fall is a possible bottom, the same shape after a rise is a “hanging man”. Traders usually wait for the next candle to confirm before acting.

Point & Figure patterns: a cheatsheet

Point & Figure charts have a simpler, more mechanical vocabulary. The basic signal is a breakout: a buy signal when a column of X’s rises one box above the top of the previous X column, and a sell signal when a column of O’s falls one box below the bottom of the previous O column. The other patterns are variations on that, with more columns testing the same level before it breaks.

Patterns are probabilities, not promisesEvery pattern here fails often, and on a finished chart it is easy to remember the ones that worked. Traders who use them typically look for a pattern at a meaningful level (an old high, a long-term average), on rising volume, and confirmed by the next candle or column, and they decide in advance where they will get out if it fails.

Part 3Moving averages and crosses

The simplest technical tool is the moving average: the average closing price over the last so many days, recalculated each day so it slides along with the price. A 50-day average smooths out daily noise; a 200-day average shows the long-term trend. When the price is above its 200-day average, many traders treat the trend as up.

The most famous signals come from two averages crossing. A golden cross is when the 50-day average rises above the 200-day: the recent trend has turned up strongly enough to lift the short average over the long one, often read as the start of a major rally. A death cross is the opposite, the 50-day falling below the 200-day. Because averages lag the price, both signals arrive after the turn has already begun.

Part 4Momentum studies

Beyond moving averages sits a long menu of studies, the indicators a charting app offers under a button. Most fall into four families. Momentum oscillators measure how fast prices are moving and flag when a move may be overstretched. Moving-average tools such as MACD measure the gap between a fast and a slow average. Volume and money-flow indicators weigh price moves by how much was traded, on the idea that a rise on heavy volume means more than one on light volume. Bands draw a channel around the price to show when it has strayed far from its average.

Two ideas run through almost all of them. Overbought and oversold: when an oscillator reaches an extreme, the move may be due a pause. And divergence: when the price makes a new high but the indicator doesn’t, the move may be losing strength. Pick a family to see its members.

Part 5Does any of it work?

This is where economists and traders have argued for decades. The case against: if past prices reliably predicted future ones, traders would exploit the pattern until it disappeared. That is the idea behind the efficient-market view, and it is why many academics long dismissed chart-reading.

The evidence is more mixed than either side admits. In 1992 William Brock3, Josef Lakonishok and Blake LeBaron tested simple moving-average and breakout rules on the Dow Jones index from 1897 to 1986 and found they beat holding cash, with prices falling on average after sell signals. Later researchers pushed back: some found the profits would have been eaten by trading costs, others that testing hundreds of rules and keeping the winners, data snooping4, explains much of the result, and a 2015 study found the predictive power of moving averages had faded in the decades after the paper was published.

There is also a self-fulfilling side. When enough traders watch the same 200-day average or the same round-number level, their orders can cluster there and make the level matter, for a while. And charts can be useful without predicting anything: as a way to see the trend at a glance, to set a stop-loss, or to avoid buying into a move that has already run a long way.

Three trapsHindsight: on a finished chart every signal looks obvious; in real time, most crosses and breakouts fail or arrive late. Overfitting: tune enough settings on past data and something will always look brilliant, which is why tests on data the rule has never seen matter. Costs: frequent trading pays the spread and fees every time, as the order book in the forex explainer shows.

Used sensibly, technical analysis is a way of reading the crowd, the same thing retail sentiment data tries to do. It tells you what prices have done and how traders are positioned; what the business is worth is a separate question, and the long-run evidence in The Saver’s Ladder suggests that for most people, owning the whole market cheaply beats trying to time it.
