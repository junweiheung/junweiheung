Three Kingdoms

In 184 a religious rising shook the Han empire, and the generals who put it down never gave their armies back. For the next century they and their heirs fought over China, until three states were left standing: Wei, Shu and Wu. Scroll through the map as the borders move, and follow one life through it.

This follows the history, not the novel. From 155 to 220 the timeline goes year by year through the Annals of Emperor Wu, Cao Cao's own chapter in Chen Shou's Records of the Three Kingdoms (written in the 280s); after 220 it follows the other annals and biographies. Each step quotes the original text and says where it comes from. Stories that appear only in Pei Songzhi's fifth-century notes, or in later histories such as the Book of the Later Han and the Book of Jin, are labelled as such, and where the sources disagree, as over the death of Xun Yu, both versions are given. Borders are simplified to the Han provinces. Years are Western; months are the Chinese lunar months, so the twelfth month of a year often falls early in the next Western year.

Key findings

  • The Han dynasty did not fall to the Yellow Turban rising of 184. It fell to the generals who put the rising down and kept their armies.
  • The north was settled at Guandu in 200, when Cao Cao beat Yuan Shao. The split of China was settled at Red Cliffs in 208, when Sun Quan and Liu Bei beat Cao Cao.
  • The three-way division hardened in 219, when Sun Quan broke his alliance with Liu Bei and seized Jing province. In 220–222 all three founders' houses declared themselves emperors.
  • None of the three founders' families won. The Sima family took over Wei from within, and Sima Yi's grandson reunited China as the Jin dynasty in 280.

Part 1An empire comes apart

By the late second century the Han dynasty had ruled China for almost four hundred years. Its court was hollowing out: emperors came to the throne as children, and palace eunuchs and the families of empresses fought over who governed in their name. In the countryside, floods, epidemics and land-grabbing landlords drove farmers into debt and onto the roads.

In 184 a healer named Zhang Jue turned that misery into a movement. His followers, the Yellow Turbans, rose across eastern China on the same day. The rising was crushed within a year, but to do it the court had let provincial officers and local strongmen raise armies of their own. When the capital itself fell into chaos in 189, those armies became the real power in the land. What followed was not one war but a century of them.

Part 2The cast

Six people carry the story here. Three founded states: Cao Cao in the north, Liu Bei in the west and Sun Quan in the south-east. Zhuge Liang and Guan Yu served Liu Bei. Sima Yi served the Cao family, and his family ended up taking their throne. Pick anyone on the chart to follow them through the timeline.

Part 4History and the novel

Most people meet this period through Romance of the Three Kingdoms, a novel put together around the 14th century and usually credited to Luo Guanzhong. It keeps the outline of events but turns Liu Bei into a saintly hero, Cao Cao into a villain and Zhuge Liang into something close to a wizard. Some of its best-known scenes are inventions or borrowings.

Part 5What it cost

The human cost was enormous, though hard to count. The Han census of 157 registered about 56 million people. When Jin counted its newly united empire in 280, it registered about 16 million. Not all of that gap was death: war and famine killed millions, but many more slipped off the registers as they fled, or were taken on as tenants and soldiers by great families whom no tax collector could touch. Either way, the state that came out of the war was far weaker than the one that went in.

The unity didn't last. Within a generation of 280 the Jin princes were fighting each other, northern peoples overran the old heartland, and China split north from south for almost three centuries. The Three Kingdoms became the stuff of storytelling instead: in plays, the novel, and now films and video games. The real history is stranger, and often harsher, than the story.

The Han was not toppled by its rebels. It was dismantled by the generals it sent to fight them.

Where the history comes from.

  • The main source for the events, places, quotations and lives here: Chen Shou, Records of the Three Kingdoms (Sanguozhi), especially the Annals of Emperor Wu, with Pei Songzhi's fifth-century annotations. Full Chinese text: Chinese Text Project, Diancang and Chinese Notes; modern Chinese translation: 211cn.
  • Events recorded outside the Records: Fan Ye, Book of the Later Han (Tao Qian, Kong Rong, Empress Fu), and the Book of Jin, Annals of Emperor Xuan (Sima Yi).
  • Cao Cao's life and career, and how history, anecdote and fiction diverge: Rafe de Crespigny, Imperial Warlord: A Biography of Cao Cao 155–220 AD (Brill, 2010).
  • The Sun family and the founding of Wu: Rafe de Crespigny, Generals of the South (ANU, 1990; open access).
  • The dates of the three states, the fall of Shu in 263 and the founding of Jin: Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Three Kingdoms".
  • The registered populations of 157 and 280: the treatises on commanderies in the Book of the Later Han and on geography in the Book of Jin, as discussed in de Crespigny's work above.
  • The novel and its relationship to the history: Columbia University, World Epics: Three Kingdoms.
  • Province outlines on the map are simplified from historical atlases of the late Han and drawn by hand; they show which province each power held, not precise frontiers.

Ages are approximate: Chinese sources count age differently, and some birth years are uncertain or unknown.
