The Domino Year

In July 1997 Thailand ran out of dollars defending its currency and let the baht float. It fell, and the panic jumped from one Asian economy to the next: Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, then South Korea, in a matter of months. The IMF arrived with the largest rescues in its history and conditions that still divide economists. Here is how the dominoes fell, what the bailouts asked for, and why one country refused the deal.

This is a plain-English history of a complex, multi-country crisis, with rounded figures and simplified causes; economists still disagree about what mattered most and whether the IMF's response helped or hurt. The map is a stylised sequence of the contagion, not a day-by-day record, and the currency falls are rounded, measured from July 1997 to January 1998. Nothing here is investment advice.

Key findings

  • Several Asian economies had boomed on foreign money, much of it short-term dollar loans, while pegging their currencies to the US dollar. That left them exposed if the dollars ever left.
  • In July 1997 Thailand ran down its reserves defending the baht, then let it float. It crashed, and firms that had borrowed in dollars were suddenly crushed by their debts.
  • The panic spread by contagion: investors saw the same weaknesses next door and pulled money out. Within months Malaysia, the Philippines, Indonesia (rupiah down about 80%) and then South Korea were hit, Korea taking the largest IMF rescue ever at the time (~$58B).
  • The IMF's bailouts came with austerity, high interest rates, and bank and governance reform. Supporters say they restored confidence; critics say the tight policies deepened the slump. Malaysia refused the IMF and used capital controls instead.

Part 1The setup

Through the early 1990s, Southeast and East Asia were the world's growth story. Thailand, Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines and South Korea pulled in huge amounts of foreign capital chasing high returns. Much of it arrived as short-term dollar loans: cheap to borrow, but repayable fast and in a currency these countries didn't print.

To make the borrowing feel safe, several governments pegged their currencies to the US dollar, holding, say, the baht at a roughly fixed rate. A stable peg told foreign lenders they wouldn't lose money on exchange-rate swings. Companies borrowed dollars happily and poured the money into property and expansion. The weakness was hidden in plain sight: if confidence ever cracked and everyone tried to swap local currency back into dollars at once, the peg would need more dollars than the central bank had.

Part 2Thailand breaks first

Thailand was the first domino. Its exports had slowed, its property boom was wobbling, and traders began to doubt the baht's peg. In the first half of 1997, speculators attacked the baht, selling it hard and betting Thailand couldn't hold the line.

To defend the peg, Thailand's central bank had to buy its own currency with dollars, spending down its foreign reserves and raising interest rates. For a while it worked. But the reserves ran low, and on 2 July 1997 Thailand gave up and let the baht float. It immediately sank.

The reserves were also smaller than they looked. On paper the Bank of Thailand still had about $31 billion in June 1997. But it had quietly promised to hand over $23.4 billion to other banks in the coming year through forward contracts, deals it had used to defend the baht without the reserve figure visibly falling. When it finally disclosed them in August, it was clear how little had really been left to fight with.

Part 3The contagion

What turned one country's problem into a regional crisis was contagion: once the baht broke, investors looked at Thailand's neighbours and saw the same setup, short-term dollar debt behind a shaky peg, and rushed to pull their money out before those currencies fell too. The selling became self-fulfilling. Watch it spread.

Part 4Five economies, one panic

The same shock hit each country differently, depending on how much dollar debt it carried and how the government reacted. Start with the currencies themselves.

Part 5The IMF bailout

As currencies and banks collapsed, Thailand, Indonesia and South Korea turned to the International Monetary Fund, the lender of last resort for countries that run out of foreign money. The IMF put together emergency loans on a scale never seen before: around $17 billion for Thailand (August 1997), $23 billion for Indonesia (November 1997) and a record ~$58 billion for South Korea (December 1997). These were packages, not just IMF money: the Fund's own share was between about a quarter and a half, with the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank and other governments adding the rest.

The totals aren't quite like for like. Korea's ~$58 billion counts the backup money other governments pledged. Indonesia's $23 billion is only its "first line"; the US, Japan, Singapore and others pledged a "second line of defence" on top (the US alone offered $3 billion), which isn't in that figure.

The money came with strings, the IMF's conditionality. In exchange for the loans, governments had to adopt a standard package of reforms meant to restore confidence and fix the weaknesses that caused the crisis.

Part 6What the conditions did

Here the story splits. The IMF's defenders argue the rescues stopped the free-fall: once credible money and reform were in place, currencies stabilised and capital slowly returned. The economies that took the deal did recover, some within a couple of years.

Critics argue the medicine made the patient sicker. Austerity and sky-high interest rates hit economies that were already collapsing, turning a currency crisis into a deep recession: firms that might have survived went under, unemployment jumped, and in Indonesia the hardship spilled into riots and the fall of a government. Many economists later agreed the IMF applied a template built for over-spending governments to a crisis that was really about private short-term debt and panic, and that the tight policies were, at least at first, the wrong tool.

Everyone agreed the fire had to be put out. The fight was over whether the IMF used water or petrol.

Part 7Did the bailout work?

The same template met four different economies and produced four different results, and the gap between Indonesia and South Korea shows that what mattered was often not the conditions on paper but how, and whether, they were carried out. Korea owned its reforms and executed them fast; Indonesia's programme stalled on politics and reversals. The clearest way to see it is country by country: what each received, what it was told to do, how it actually carried that out, how deep the 1998 recession ran, and the verdict with hindsight. Pick a country.

Part 8Malaysia's different path

One country said no. Malaysia refused the IMF and its conditions. Instead of raising rates and opening up, Prime Minister Mahathir Mohamad's government did the opposite. On 1 September 1998 it imposed sweeping capital controls: money invested in Malaysia had to stay at least a year, and the offshore ringgit market, where speculators had been borrowing ringgit to bet against it, was shut down. The next day it fixed the ringgit at 3.80 to the dollar.

With money unable to rush out, Malaysia could cut interest rates and spend to support the economy, the opposite of the IMF's recipe. The peg held for almost seven years, until July 2005, when Malaysia moved to a managed float the same day China revalued the yuan.

At the time, this was treated as heresy, and many warned it would scare off investors for good. In the event, Malaysia's downturn was real but its recovery held up, and its controls are now seen, at least by some, as a defensible way to buy time and stop a panic, rather than the disaster they were billed as. It's the clearest natural experiment the crisis offered: the same shock, a very different response.

Part 9Who escaped, and why

Not everyone fell. Several of the region's economies, China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Singapore and Japan, came through with only a brush rather than a collapse. None was immune: all lost some growth as regional demand and confidence drained away. But none suffered a currency-and-banking meltdown, and the reasons are the mirror image of what sank the others.

The common thread is telling. The economies that escaped either didn't let in the short-term dollar money in the first place (China's closed account), or held enough reserves and ran their currencies carefully enough that a run never started (Taiwan, Singapore), or had the reserves and the rules to win the fight when one did (Hong Kong). It's the same lesson the hit countries learned the hard way, seen from the other side.

Part 10The lessons

The crisis reshaped how Asia manages money. Burned by the experience, many of the hit economies spent the next decade building huge foreign-exchange reserves, a self-insurance stockpile of dollars so they'd never again be at the mercy of the IMF or a sudden outflow. The region grew warier of short-term foreign debt and fixed pegs.

The deeper lesson is about how fast confidence travels. Nothing about Indonesia or Korea changed overnight in 1997; what changed was belief. Once investors decided Asian pegs were fragile, the rush for the exits made the fear come true, country after country. A crisis that began with one currency in Bangkok became a regional collapse because money, and panic, move together.
