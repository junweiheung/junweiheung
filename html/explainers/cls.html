The Bank Behind the Currency Market

Swap one currency for another and two payments must cross two countries on two different clocks. If one arrives and the other doesn't, you have lost the full amount. One bank you have almost certainly never heard of, CLS, exists to stop that. It settles 18 currencies, the Singapore dollar among them. Yet only about a third of the market's daily settlement goes through that kind of lock.

This is about one corner of finance: how foreign-exchange (FX) trades are settled, and the single institution, CLS, built to make that safe. Most figures come from the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) and from CLS's own pages, which are the source with an interest in the story. A few historical details and records come from Wikipedia and are marked as such. The BIS figures are for one month (April 2025) and its authors warn the 2025 method is not comparable with the 2019 and 2022 surveys. CLS's operating window is from a 2015 Reserve Bank of Australia paper plus a CLS description, so check it against current CLS rules. Conversions to Singapore dollars use about S$1.29 per US$1 (20 Nov 2026) and are rounded. This explains an industry; it isn't investment or financial advice.

Key findings

  • A currency trade has two payments in two countries. If one side pays and the other doesn't, the loser is out the full amount. That is "settlement risk", and it has a name: Herstatt risk, after a German bank that closed on 26 June 1974.
  • More than US$14 trillion (about S$18tn) of FX obligations came due on an average day in April 2025. Only 36% (about $5.2tn) settled "payment versus payment", the lock that CLS provides. About 10%, or $1.4tn, had no protection at all.
  • CLS, a bank with no household customers, settles 18 currencies using accounts at 18 central banks. It says it settles about US$8tn a day and cuts funding needs by more than 96%.
  • CLS can't cover everything: pairs it doesn't handle are only 12% locked, and the Indonesian rupiah, Malaysian ringgit and Thai baht, among others, sit outside it. Singapore's dollar is inside.

Part 1The bank you can't bank with

Start with why you have never heard of it. Whenever a company swaps Singapore dollars for US dollars, or a fund hedges a euro position, the trade is agreed in seconds on a screen. That is the easy part. The hard part comes after: two payments, in two currencies, through two countries' payment systems that open and close at different hours. The agreeing is famous. The moving of the money is plumbing.

The plumbing has a name: CLS Bank International, a New York institution regulated by the US Federal Reserve, according to CLS. Ordinary people and firms can't open an account there. Its direct customers are about 75 big banks ("settlement members"); another roughly 38,000 participants use it through them. CLS itself says it settles about US$8tn a day (about S$10tn). For scale, the BIS counted US$9.6tn a day of FX trading in April 2025 (BIS release). The two numbers measure different things (settled value is not the same as trading turnover), so don't divide one by the other.

Part 2The afternoon Herstatt closed

Here is the story the whole system is built on. On 26 June 1974, German regulators shut Bankhaus Herstatt at 3.30pm Frankfurt time. Earlier that day, other banks had paid Herstatt Deutsche marks in Frankfurt for US dollars they expected in New York. But New York was hours behind. Herstatt's New York correspondent suspended its outgoing dollar payments, and the banks that had already paid their marks got nothing back. That account is in the BIS Quarterly Review, June 2026.

The lesson isn't about one weak bank. It is about time zones. Any trade where you pay first and receive later leaves you exposed for the gap in between, and the exposure is the whole trade, not a small fee. The BIS describes it as the full value for the period between delivering one currency and receiving the other. Banks call it Herstatt risk, and for decades it hung over the FX market. A 1996 report from the central-bank community (the Allsopp report, per Wikipedia) called time on it, and the banks eventually built their own fix.

Part 3The fix: lock the swap

CLS was the banks' answer. Wikipedia dates the Allsopp report to 1996, the company to 1997, CLS Bank to 1999, and the first live settlement to 9 September 2002 with seven currencies. Today CLS holds an account at the central bank of each currency it handles, so both halves of a trade can move at once, inside CLS's books. A 2015 Reserve Bank of Australia paper describes a daily window of 07:00 to 12:00 Central European Time for most currencies, shorter (07:00 to 10:00 CET) for the Asia-Pacific ones. In Singapore's winter clock that's roughly 2pm to 7pm (my arithmetic, so check it against CLS's current rules).

That's the first trick: the lock. The second is netting: instead of sending every payment, CLS adds up what each bank owes and is owed in each currency and moves only the difference. CLS says this cuts the funding banks need by more than 96% on average. That figure is CLS's own claim.

Even in the most settled market on earth, one trade in ten still has the Herstatt gap wide open.

Part 5Why it's a bottleneck

A lock only helps on the doors it fits. Three things stop the lock from reaching every trade.

The currency has to be one of the 18. Trades in currencies CLS can handle came to $12.2tn a day, more than four-fifths of the market, yet only 40% of them actually used the lock. For pairs CLS doesn't cover, almost nothing was locked: just 12% went through other lock-style systems. Those other systems are CCIL in India, CHATS in Hong Kong and Buna in the UAE, according to the BIS.

Both sides have to be set up for it. Of the unprotected 10%, about three-quarters could not have used the lock even if they'd wanted to. The most common reason, the BIS says, was that one side of the trade simply wasn't connected to any locking system (57% of cases). And the trade has to be the right kind. Trades that settle the same day can't use the lock yet, and that explains 37% of the stuck trades; another 36% were in currencies the lock doesn't cover. (A trade can hit more than one of these, so the reasons add up to more than 100%.) The last quarter, $347bn a day, could have been locked and wasn't: the firms just didn't use it.

And there is the bigger worry. CLS isn't one lock among many: it's the single hub the market built, and regulators treat it that way. Wikipedia reports that in July 2012 a US regulator formally labelled CLS "systemically important", official code for too central to be allowed to fail. When the lock is one institution, "what happens if the lock itself breaks?" is a fair question. I found no record of a serious outage in these sources, but that's silence in what I read, not proof it can't happen.

Part 6The Singapore angle

Singapore is a major FX trading centre, and the Singapore dollar is one of CLS's 18. Wikipedia dates its addition to September 2003, in the second wave after the original seven, and the Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) is the central bank holding CLS's SGD account. So an SGD/USD trade between two members can settle with the lock.

The neighbours are a different story. The rupiah, ringgit and baht aren't CLS currencies. According to the BIS they are settled PvP through CHATS in Hong Kong, a system that has existed since 2000, or with less protection. For a Singapore treasury, the practical lesson is to know which of your currency pairs can be locked, and which depend on trust and timing. Asia-Pacific currencies' earlier pay-in window (2015 figures, see above) also means a late Asian afternoon is when the clock matters most.

So the bottleneck isn't a mine or a fab. It is a lock: the one institution that ties 18 central banks' clocks together for a few hours each day, so that nobody gets Herstatted. Next time you hear about a currency trade, ask two things.

QUESTION 1

Why do two-thirds of the market still settle without the lock, and who bears the cost when a leg fails?

QUESTION 2

What happens to currencies outside the 18 if shorter settlement cycles and same-day trading squeeze the time to fix errors?
