Ask Why

Enron's slogan was "Ask why". For six years running, Fortune named it America's most innovative company. In 2001 it went bankrupt, then the largest bankruptcy in American history, and took its auditor down with it. How a gas-pipeline company turned into a trading house, booked profits it hadn't earned, hid losses in partnerships run by its own finance chief, and fell apart in a few months when people finally asked.

By Jun Wei Heung · Words and graphics
November 28, 2026 · 18 min read

This summarises the Enron scandal from the company's founding in 1985 to the trials that followed its collapse. The structure follows a Chinese-language video explainer1; every fact has been checked against court records, regulators and contemporary reporting, and where the video dramatised or got something wrong, this page uses the record. Ken Lay's conviction was vacated after his death, and Arthur Andersen's conviction was overturned on appeal; both are noted. The two interactive charts use invented round numbers to show how the accounting worked. This is educational, not investment advice.

Key findings

  • Enron began in 1985 as a merger of two gas-pipeline companies. Under Jeffrey Skilling it became a trading house that bought and sold gas, power and much else, and by 2000 it reported revenue of about $101 billion.
  • Mark-to-market accounting, approved by the SEC in 1992, let Enron book the whole estimated profit of a long-term deal on the day it was signed. When deals earned less than hoped, Enron needed ever more new deals to cover the gap.
  • Chief financial officer Andrew Fastow ran partnerships that bought Enron's troubled assets and "hedged" its investments using, in effect, Enron's own shares. They hid losses only as long as the share price stayed high.
  • In 2001 the questions started: a Fortune article asking how Enron made money, Skilling's sudden resignation, an internal warning, then a $618 million loss and a $586 million restatement. Enron filed for bankruptcy on 2 December 2001.
  • Fastow got six years and Skilling 24 years, later cut to 14. Ken Lay was convicted but died before sentencing. Arthur Andersen collapsed after being convicted of obstruction, a verdict the Supreme Court overturned in 2005. The scandal led directly to the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002.

Part 1A pipeline company

Enron was born in 1985 out of a merger between Houston Natural Gas and InterNorth, a pipeline company from Nebraska. Kenneth Lay, Houston Natural Gas's chief executive, soon ran the combined company. He had a doctorate in economics, had served as a naval officer in the Pentagon and as a senior energy official in Washington, and kept close ties to politicians, including the Bush family2.

For its first years Enron did something unglamorous: it owned pipelines and moved natural gas. But America was deregulating its gas and power markets, letting prices move with supply and demand, and Lay saw a chance to do more than move the stuff.

1985

Two pipelines become one

Houston Natural Gas merges with InterNorth of Omaha. Ken Lay soon runs the combined company, renamed Enron. It owns pipelines and moves natural gas, a dull, steady business, just as Washington begins letting gas prices float.

1987

The first warning

Enron's oil-trading unit in Valhalla, New York, has been making unusually large profits. Its two top traders turn out to be running unauthorised bets and diverting money into their own accounts. By one account the bets could have cost Enron about $1 billion before a colleague unwound them; the reported loss is $85 million after tax4.

Both traders later plead guilty, and one goes to prison. Accounts differ on how much Lay knew, but the unit had kept trading after the first red flags.

1990

Skilling's Gas Bank

Lay hires Jeffrey Skilling, a Harvard MBA who, as a McKinsey consultant, had advised Enron to set up a "Gas Bank". Enron would buy gas from producers and sell it to utilities and factories under long-term contracts at fixed prices, trading the risk in between: in effect, a dealer in forward contracts.

January 1992

The accounting change

The SEC allows Enron to use mark-to-market accounting for its gas contracts: the profit expected over a contract's whole life can be booked the day it's signed5. Part 3 shows what that does to the numbers.

1996–1999

America's most innovative company

Skilling takes the trading model everywhere: electricity, coal, paper, steel, even weather. Because Enron counts the full value of each trade as revenue, not just its margin, revenue soars. Fortune names it "America's most innovative company" every year from 1996.

Inside, employees are ranked against each other every year, and the bottom group, often said to be about 15%, is pushed out. What counts is the deals you book, and the profit they show on paper.

1998–1999

Fastow's partnerships

Andrew Fastow becomes chief financial officer in 1998 and starts setting up partnerships, such as LJM, that he runs himself. They buy Enron's troubled assets and do deals with it, keeping losses and debt off its books. Part 4 explains how.

The shares split two-for-one in August 1999 and end the year at $44.38, up from $28.53 a year earlier.

April 2000

Films down the wire

Enron's broadband unit signs a 20-year deal with Blockbuster to stream films to homes. The service barely works and ends within a year, but Enron uses a partnership deal to book about $111 million of profit from it6. Prosecutors later charge executives over it.

23 August 2000

The peak

Enron's shares close at $90, valuing it at about $70 billion. For 2000 it will report revenue of about $101 billion, seventh in the Fortune 500. Behind the paper profits, real investments are failing: a giant power plant at Dabhol in India, a water business called Azurix, the broadband unit.

2000–2001

California

California has partly deregulated electricity. Prices soar, rolling blackouts hit, and the state's biggest utility, PG&E, files for bankruptcy in April 2001. Enron's traders profit, using strategies called "Death Star" and "Get Shorty" to game the market's rules. Recordings released in 2004 catch them joking about the money taken from "Grandma Millie"7.

February 2001

"Is Enron overpriced?"

Skilling becomes chief executive. Fortune's Bethany McLean asks a simple question: how exactly does Enron make its money? Few analysts can say, and short sellers are already betting against the stock8. The shares close at $75.09 on 20 February.

17 April 2001

The analyst call

On a call with analysts, a fund manager presses Skilling on why Enron hasn't released a balance sheet with its earnings. Skilling calls him an obscene name.

14 August 2001

Skilling quits

After six months as chief executive, Skilling resigns, citing personal reasons. The shares close at $39.55, less than half their peak. Lay returns as CEO and tells employees the stock is a bargain9.

15 August 2001

The memo

Sherron Watkins, a vice-president, sends Lay an anonymous memo warning that Enron could "implode in a wave of accounting scandals". She later meets him in person10.

16 October 2001

The loss

Enron reports a $618 million quarterly loss and a $1.2 billion cut to shareholders' equity, much of it from the partnerships. The shares close at $33.84. Six days later the SEC opens an inquiry.

26–30 October 2001

The partnerships come out

The Wall Street Journal reports on Chewco, another Fastow partnership. The shares fall to $15.40, and on 30 October close at $11.16, a nine-year low. Fastow has already been replaced as finance chief.

8 November 2001

The restatement

Enron restates its results for 1997 to 2000, cutting reported profits by about $586 million. Years of growth were partly invented.

26–28 November 2001

Junk

The shares close at $4.01 on 26 November. Two days later a planned rescue merger with Dynegy collapses, rating agencies cut Enron to junk, and billions of dollars of debt fall due at once. The shares fall below $1.

2 December 2001

Bankruptcy

Enron files for bankruptcy, its last close 26 cents. With $63.4 billion of assets, it is then the largest bankruptcy in US history11. About 20,000 people lose their jobs, and many lose retirement savings held in Enron stock.

The end ran like a bank run: Enron's trading depended on borrowed money and on partners trusting it to pay. Once its rating fell, they demanded cash, and there wasn't enough.

Part 3Profits from the future

Paper profits are the heart of the story. Most companies record profit as they earn it: sign a ten-year contract, and you book each year's profit in that year. In 1992 the SEC allowed Enron to use mark-to-market accounting for its gas contracts instead, as banks do for securities. Under mark-to-market, a contract is recorded at its estimated current value, so the profit expected over its whole life can be booked on the day it's signed.

For a bank holding traded bonds, the value comes from market prices. For a 20-year power contract with no market price, the value came from Enron's own forecasts. If a deal later earned less than forecast, Enron had to record the shortfall as a loss, unless it could book enough new deals to cover it. The chart shows why that became a treadmill.

The trap is in the timing. Year one looks spectacular, but not a dollar of that profit has arrived as cash. Every later year starts with a hole. To keep reported earnings growing, Enron had to sign bigger deals each year, each booking its own forecast profit up front. Its earnings kept rising; its cash didn't.

Part 4The partnerships

Mark-to-market inflated profits. The second trick hid losses and debt. Andrew Fastow, a finance specialist Skilling brought in who became chief financial officer in 1998, set up hundreds of special purpose entities: separate companies that could buy assets from Enron, borrow money or enter into deals with it. Under the accounting rules of the time, if outsiders put in at least 3% of an entity's equity and bore real risk, Enron didn't have to include it in its own accounts.

Fastow ran several of these partnerships himself, called LJM, Chewco and a set known as the Raptors, and was paid millions of dollars in fees by them. An investigation commissioned by Enron's own board, the Powers report, and later the SEC found that the outside investment was often not really at risk, that Enron had secretly promised to protect investors, and that the deals let Enron hide losses and debt while Fastow and a few colleagues profited12.

Part 5The reckoning

Fastow pleaded guilty and became the government's key witness. In 2006 he was sentenced to six years in prison, less than the ten his plea deal had pointed to, and he was released in 2011. Lay and Skilling were convicted of fraud and conspiracy in May 2006. Lay died of heart disease that July, before he could be sentenced, and his conviction was vacated because he could no longer appeal. Skilling was sentenced to 24 years; in 2013 the sentence was cut to 14, and he was released in 2019 after about twelve years in custody13.

Enron's auditor, Arthur Andersen, one of the five largest accounting firms in the world, was convicted in 2002 of obstructing justice by destroying Enron documents. The firm gave up its licence to audit public companies and collapsed; tens of thousands of staff worldwide lost their jobs, and the "Big Five" became the Big Four. In 2005 the Supreme Court unanimously overturned the conviction, because the jury had been wrongly instructed. By then there was no firm left to save14.

Congress responded with the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002. Chief executives and finance chiefs must now personally certify their company's accounts, auditors are overseen by a new public board and limited in the consulting they can sell to the firms they audit, and companies must test their internal controls15.

Enron's slogan was "Ask why". The people who asked, a reporter, a few short sellers and one of its own vice-presidents, were right.

The video this page draws on ends with a string of questions. Why did Lay let the Valhalla traders keep going? Why did Skilling build a system that rewarded booking deals over making money? Why did Fastow turn his talent to hiding losses, the auditors sign off, and the analysts keep saying buy? There's no single answer. But the warning signs were there for anyone who asked how Enron's profits turned into cash. Mostly, they didn't.
