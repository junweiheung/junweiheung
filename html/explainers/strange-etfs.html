An ETF for Every Theory

Exchange-traded funds began as a cheap way to own the whole market. Now there's one for almost any idea: copying Congress, backing a party, betting against a famous fund manager or a TV stock-picker, or trading fear itself. A tour of the strangest, what's inside them, and the boring fund they're all up against.

Key findings

  • An ETF is a basket of investments that trades on an exchange like a single share. The cheapest track a whole index: Vanguard's S&P 500 fund, VOO, charges 0.03% a year.
  • For a Singapore investor, where a fund is based matters as much as what it holds. US-domiciled funds like VOO face 30% US tax on dividends and US estate tax above $60,000; Irish-domiciled funds like VWRA pay 15% on US dividends and avoid the estate-tax exposure.
  • NANC and GOP copy the disclosed trades of Democratic and Republican members of Congress. NANC has beaten the S&P 500 since 2023, mostly through its tilt to big tech, at about 25 times VOO's fee. DEMZ picks companies by their political giving.
  • Funds built to bet against someone have had mixed fortunes: SARK, the inverse of Cathie Wood's ARK Innovation fund, more than doubled in its first year, while the Inverse Cramer ETF lost 15% and closed after eleven months.
  • Products tracking the VIX "fear index" hold futures, not the index, and lose value steadily when futures are priced above today's level. One that bet against volatility, XIV, lost more than 90% in a single day in February 2018 and was shut down.

Part 1What an ETF is

An exchange-traded fund pools investors' money into a basket of assets, shares, bonds or something more exotic, and lists that basket on a stock exchange. You buy and sell it during the day like a single share. Most ETFs track an index, mechanically holding whatever the index holds; some are actively managed; and some, known as leveraged or inverse funds, use derivatives to deliver a multiple or the opposite of an index's daily move1.

The fund charges an annual fee, the expense ratio, taken quietly out of its value. Because an ETF can be built around any rule someone can write down, the fund industry has turned the format into a way to sell almost any idea. Here are some of the strangest, and the plain fund they all have to beat.

Part 2The boring benchmark

VOO, the Vanguard S&P 500 ETF, holds the 500 large American companies in the S&P 500 index, in proportion to their size, and charges 0.03% a year: $3 on every $10,0002. It does nothing clever, and that's the point. Most professional fund managers fail to beat the index over long periods, so a cheap fund that simply owns it is the yardstick every other fund on this page should be judged against. (How the index decides who gets the most weight is in a separate explainer.)

VWRA, the Vanguard FTSE All-World UCITS ETF, goes further: it owns about 4,000 companies across developed and emerging markets, and reinvests its dividends rather than paying them out. In July 2026 Vanguard cut its fee from 0.19% to 0.14%3. It's a favourite of Singapore investors, and the reason has less to do with what it owns than with where it's based.

VOO is a US fund. A Singapore investor who holds it has 30% of its dividends withheld as US tax, since Singapore has no tax treaty with the US, and, as a non-US person, could face US estate tax of up to 40% on US-based assets above just $60,000 on death. VWRA is domiciled in Ireland. Under the Ireland–US tax treaty, the fund pays 15% on the dividends it receives from US companies, and as an Irish fund it isn't a US asset for estate tax4.

Part 3Trading like Congress

Members of the US Congress may own and trade shares, but since the 2012 STOCK Act they must publicly report each trade, generally within 45 days. Suspicions that some lawmakers trade on what they learn in office have made those reports a minor obsession online. In February 2023 the data site Unusual Whales turned the obsession into two funds5:

Democrats

NANC

The Unusual Whales Subversive Democratic Trading ETF buys what Democratic members of Congress and their spouses report buying. Its ticker is a nod to Nancy Pelosi, whose husband's trades are the most watched. It has leaned heavily to big tech.

Fee: about 0.74% a year, roughly 25 times VOO's.
Republicans

GOP

The Republican twin copies Republican lawmakers' disclosed trades. It launched with the ticker KRUZ, after Senator Ted Cruz, before switching to GOP. Its top holdings have included a bank, telecom and energy companies alongside Nvidia.

Fee: about 0.74% a year.

Has it worked? NANC has: one report put its return from launch to May 2026 at about 93%, against about 79% for the S&P 500, and in 2025 both funds beat the index6. But analysts who've looked inside mostly put NANC's lead down to its heavy weighting in technology shares, which led the market, rather than to any secret knowledge. The trades it copies are also weeks old by the time they're disclosed. And the funds face a political risk of their own: bills to ban members of Congress from trading shares at all keep coming back, and one passing would remove the funds' reason to exist7.

A third political fund takes a different route. DEMZ, the Democratic Large Cap Core ETF, launched in November 2020, holds large US companies whose employees' political donations lean strongly towards Democrats, and charges 0.45%8. It's a fund for investors who want their money to match their politics, more than for those hunting an edge.

Part 4Betting against someone

If you can build a fund to follow someone, you can build one to bet against them. The first target was Cathie Wood, whose ARK Innovation ETF (ARKK) soared in 2020 on bets on companies such as Tesla, Zoom and Coinbase, and then fell hard. In November 2021 Tuttle Capital launched SARK, designed to deliver the opposite of ARKK's return each day, using swaps rather than short selling. It was the first US ETF built to deliver the inverse of another ETF9.

Its timing was perfect. As ARKK lost about two-thirds of its value in 2022, SARK rose more than 110% in its first year. It has since changed hands and names twice, becoming the AXS Short Innovation Daily ETF and, from April 2025, the Tradr 1X Short Innovation Daily ETF.

The idea's comic peak came in March 2023 with the Inverse Cramer Tracker ETF, SJIM, which bet against the stock tips of the CNBC host Jim Cramer, a long-running internet joke. The joke didn't pay. SJIM gathered only $2.4 million, lost about 15% while the S&P 500 gained about 25%, and closed in February 2024. Its sibling, which followed Cramer's picks, had already closed in 202310.

There's a catch in the small print of funds like SARK: they deliver the inverse of the daily move. Over longer periods, especially when the market swings up and down, the result can differ a lot from the inverse of the total move.

Part 5Trading fear: the VIX

The VIX, Cboe's volatility index, is often called Wall Street's fear gauge. It's calculated from the prices of options on the S&P 500 and measures how much movement traders expect over the next 30 days; it jumps when markets panic11. You can't buy the VIX itself, so products that track it, such as the iPath ETN known as VXX, hold VIX futures instead, contracts on where the index will be in a month or two.

That's the problem. Most of the time, futures are priced above today's VIX, a pattern called contango, because calm markets are expected to get a little less calm. Each month the fund sells the expiring contract and buys the next one at a higher price, and if the VIX doesn't rise, it loses the difference. Over years, long-volatility products have lost most of their value. They're built for traders hedging a few days or weeks, not for holding.

If long-volatility products bleed money, why not bet against them? For years that worked brilliantly. XIV, an exchange-traded note issued by Credit Suisse, delivered the inverse of short-term VIX futures each day, and it built a large following. Then, on 5 February 2018, the VIX had its largest one-day rise on record, about 115%. XIV's value fell more than 90% after the close, triggering a clause in its terms that let Credit Suisse wind it up. The note was shut down within weeks12. Traders called the day "Volmageddon".

Part 6What they have in common

Every fund on this page is a real product, legally sold, with a clear rulebook. But they share some patterns worth knowing before buying anything like them.

Fees

The hurdle is high

A themed fund charging 0.5–0.75% a year has to beat a 0.03% index fund by that margin every year just to tie. Over decades, as the fee chart shows, that's a big gap to close.

Design

Read the rulebook

"Inverse", "daily" and "futures-based" each mean a product that won't do what its name suggests over long periods. Many of these are built for traders holding for days, not savers holding for years.

Survival

Gimmicks close

Both Cramer funds closed within a year. XIV was terminated after one bad day. A fund that shuts returns your money at its value then, which may be well below what you paid.

The funds also say something about markets. NANC's success says more about how much of the last few years' gains came from a handful of tech giants than about Congress. SARK's first year says more about the end of a bubble than about Cathie Wood. And XIV's collapse is a reminder that a strategy that makes a little money almost every day can hide a risk that takes it all back in one.

The cleverest fund on this page has to beat the dullest one, after fees, for as long as you hold it.
