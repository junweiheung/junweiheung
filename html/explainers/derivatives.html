Financial Weapons of Mass Destruction?

A derivative is a contract whose value comes from something else: a price, a rate, an index. A farmer uses one to lock in next season's wheat price; an airline uses one to cap its fuel bill. The same contracts also let a trader bet huge sums with little money down. In 2002 Warren Buffett called them "financial weapons of mass destruction." Here is what they are, why they're useful, and how the same tools helped blow up LTCM, AIG and the 2008 crisis.

Key findings

  • A derivative is a contract that settles at a future date, and its value is derived from an underlying thing: a commodity price, an interest rate, a stock, an index.
  • Three building blocks cover most of the market: futures (fix a price now for a trade later), options (the right, not the duty, to trade at a set price), and swaps (exchange one stream of payments for another).
  • The same contract can reduce risk or add it. A user with real exposure hedges; a trader with none speculates, often with heavy leverage, controlling a large position for a small outlay.
  • Buffett's 2002 "weapons of mass destruction" warning was about that leverage plus interconnection: when big players owe each other on derivatives, one failure can cascade. LTCM (1998), AIG (2008) and the wider 2008 crisis all showed it.

Part 1What a derivative is

Buy a share of a company and you own something directly. A derivative is one step removed: it's a contract whose value is derived from the price of something else, the "underlying." The underlying can be almost anything with a price that moves: wheat, oil, a currency, an interest rate, a stock, a market index. You don't have to own the underlying to trade a derivative on it.

The common thread is time. A derivative is an agreement made today about a transaction that settles later, at a price or on terms fixed now. That lets two parties move a future risk between them: one of them wants certainty about a future price, and the other is willing to take the other side. Everything else is a variation on that idea.

Part 2The three building blocks

Most derivatives are built from three basic kinds of contract. Learn these and the rest are combinations.

Part 3Hedging vs speculation

The same contract can make the financial system safer or riskier. What changes is who's using it and why. Two people can hold the identical futures contract for opposite reasons.

A hedger already has a real-world exposure and uses a derivative to cancel it out. An airline knows it must buy jet fuel for years to come, so it buys contracts that lock in the price; if fuel spikes, the gain on the contract offsets the higher bill. A speculator has no such exposure. They take the position purely to profit from a price move, often using leverage: a small deposit controls a large contract, so gains and losses are both magnified.

A derivative doesn't create or destroy risk by itself. It moves risk from someone who wants to shed it to someone willing to carry it.

Part 4Buffett's warning

In Berkshire Hathaway's 2002 annual letter, Warren Buffett set out at length why he distrusted the fast-growing derivatives market. His line became the most quoted verdict on the subject.

"We view them as time bombs, both for the parties that deal in them and the economic system… In our view… derivatives are financial weapons of mass destruction, carrying dangers that, while now latent, are potentially lethal."
Warren Buffett, Berkshire Hathaway annual letter, 2002

His worry wasn't the contracts themselves; it was three things that come with a large, lightly watched derivatives market. Leverage lets positions grow far beyond the cash behind them. Interconnection means big institutions owe each other on these contracts, so one firm's failure becomes everyone's problem. And opacity: positions valued by models rather than market prices can hide how large and how fragile they really are, until they aren't. Within a decade, each of those played out.

Part 5When derivatives blew up

Three episodes show the failure modes Buffett named. In each, derivatives did exactly what they were designed to do until conditions turned, and the leverage and interconnection that made them powerful made the fallout spread.

Leverage

LTCM, 1998

Long-Term Capital Management, a hedge fund run by Nobel laureates, used huge leverage on derivative bets that small price gaps would close. When markets moved the wrong way, the magnified losses threatened its many trading partners.

What happened: the Federal Reserve organised a roughly $3.6 billion rescue by its lenders to unwind the positions in an orderly way and prevent a chain reaction.
Interconnection

AIG, 2008

The insurer AIG had sold vast amounts of credit default swaps, contracts promising to pay out if certain mortgage bonds defaulted. It collected premiums as if the payouts would never come, and set aside little against them.

What happened: when housing collapsed, the claims did come. AIG couldn't cover them, and because so many banks relied on its guarantees, the US committed up to $182 billion of support.
Opacity

The 2008 crisis

Mortgage risk was sliced, repackaged and insured through layers of derivatives few could value. On paper the risk looked spread out and safe; in reality it was concentrated and uncollateralised.

What happened: when the underlying mortgages failed, the hidden losses surfaced all at once across interconnected firms, turning a housing downturn into a global financial crisis.
The pattern, not the productsNote what's common across all three: the derivative worked as intended in calm times and masked a build-up of risk. The problem each time was a large, leveraged, interconnected position whose true size and fragility were hard to see from outside, exactly the "latent, potentially lethal" danger Buffett described. Used to hedge a real exposure, these same contracts are routine and useful. The danger lives in scale, leverage and opacity, not in the idea of a derivative.

Part 6Open interest

One number tells you how big and how active a derivatives market has become: open interest. It's the total count of contracts that are currently open, meaning entered into and not yet closed out, settled or expired. Every open contract has a buyer on one side and a seller on the other, so it measures the stack of live obligations between market participants.

Open interest isn't the same as volume. Volume counts how many contracts changed hands in a day, including ones opened and closed the same day; open interest counts how many remain outstanding at the end of it. A single contract can trade many times (adding to volume) without changing open interest, because it's just passing between hands. Open interest only moves when a new contract is created or an existing one is closed.

So when you read that open interest in a contract is "at a record," it means more live bets than ever are stacked up in that market, each one an obligation someone eventually has to honour. Whether that's healthy hedging or a fragile pile of speculation is the same question this whole piece turns on.

Next time you meet a derivative, ask two things.

QUESTION 1

Is this contract hedging a real risk, or creating a new one?

QUESTION 2

How much leverage, interconnection and hidden size sits behind it?
